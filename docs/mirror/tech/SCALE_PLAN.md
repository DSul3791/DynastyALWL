Author: Tyler_001, 2026-10-22, for Dave. Asked by Dave: "imagine 1000 players online, just them hitting a tree would bring it all down" (the 592 dig flood). Inputs: crash_reports/ telemetry, handoff/Willy_001_HANDOFF.md item 0, and a read of scenes/terrain_server/terrain_server.gd, src/stream/server_items.gd, terrain_net.gd, server_main.gd, app_config.gd. Numbers marked (est.) are my estimates, not measurements; step 0 of the plan is to measure them. Industry reading is cited generally (Photon MMO interest areas https://doc.photonengine.com/server/v4/applications/mmo/mmo-concept , academic surveys of MMO architectures https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Network-Architectures-and-Data-Management-for/99924463302346 , Godot ENetMultiplayerPeer docs https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.3/classes/class_enetmultiplayerpeer.html): the standard recipe is the same everywhere: server owns state, per-client rate limits, interest management, time-budgeted ticks, sharding by space, and load tests with bots.

## 0 Headline (the cynic's answer first)
1000 players cannot be served by this server as built, and no single tweak changes that. The five things that break first, in order:
1 THE HARD CAP: terrain_server.gd:392 creates the server with `create_server(port, 32)` and app_config.gd DEFAULT_MAX_CLIENTS is 32. Player 33 is refused. (Godot's ENet host allows up to 4095 peers; this is a one-line limit, but it also hides every other limit until fixed.)
2 THE ONE MAIN THREAD AND ITS BUDGET. All game logic (items, vitals, NPCs, animals, trails, water poll, RPC handling, sends) runs in terrain_server._process on one GDScript thread at the engine's frame rate. Arithmetic: if gameplay may use 25 ms of each 50 ms frame (20 Hz), that is 500 ms of main-thread time per second for everyone. Divided by 1000 players that is 0.5 ms per player per second. A dig that costs 1 s (the 592 incident), or even 5 ms, or a water poll that costs 250 ms per frame (Willy's measurement) are each hundreds of times over the budget. The server already shows it with ONE player: fps 1-12 and 1.05 s stalls in Dave's telemetry during the dig storm.
3 BROADCASTS THAT GO TO EVERYONE. These are sent with `.rpc(...)` (all peers): dug (line 988), filled (1034), flora_cut (863, 869, 954, 1022), tile_changed (972, 1193), water_changed (814), water_rate (695, 817), ground_reset (1229). One event costs N messages, and N players generating events costs N squared: 50 digs/s among 1000 players = 50,000 messages/s (est.), most to people 5 km away who do not care. tile_changed is worse: it makes every client in the world forget its prefetched blocks and re-ask (that was half of the 592 incident).
4 PER-PEER LOOPS OVER ALL PLAYERS ON THE MAIN THREAD: items._vitals_tick loops every peer every 0.25 s and runs warmth, sickness, energy and breath ticks for each (server_items.gd:3771); _send_npcs builds rows and a string signature per peer four times a second; _sync_world_items loops all items and all peers each second; items.save() builds JSON for every inventory every 30 s on the main thread. At 1000 players each of these is an O(N) pass of tens of milliseconds (est., needs measuring), and the 1.6 s "items" stall at a join (Willy) will repeat for every join.
5 NO PER-PLAYER LIMITS ANYWHERE. Every `any_peer` RPC handler runs whatever a client sends, as often as it sends (dig, cut_flora, inventory_action, trade, npc_talk, request_blocks, request_flora, request_water, admin_place). One bot or one held click is an attack. Also position is client-authoritative (Willy: the server does not validate movement), so 1000 players are 1000 trust problems.
What is NOT a first problem: bandwidth for idle or walking players (telemetry shows 4-9 packets and under 1 KB a second per idle client), the starter cache (clients ship with it), block generation after warm-up (cache hits).
DAVE'S ANSWER (2026-10-22, questions/ANSWERS.md scale-target): "1000 spread across the whole world (normal MMO target)". So the target is 1000 concurrent in the whole world, mostly spread over the tiles; layering of a single hotspot is NOT a first-phase goal. What stays: a soft per-area cap (about 150 per 256 m interest area and about 300 per tile process, est.) as a safety valve with queue/overflow into a parallel copy, because new players all spawn in the starter tile and the starter tile will still be the crowd (plan against 200-300 there, not 1000). What changes: sharding by tile (section 6) becomes the main scale-out path rather than a last step: about 9 tiles at roughly 110 players each on average, but sized for the starter tile at 2-3x; step 5 stays after steps 0-4 but is no longer optional, and design the player record store and the handoff (steps 4-5) with that in mind from the start. The original question, kept for the record:

## 1 Principles (what comparable games do, applied to us)
1 The server owns everything that matters (inventory, vitals, world items, edits, spawns, damage, trade, XP, time, loot rolls) and validates everything the client says. The client may predict and cache what is cosmetic or reversible.
2 Every client action is cheap to REJECT: validated, rate-limited and cost-charged before any real work happens. Dropping is silent and counted.
3 Fan-out is by interest: a player only receives events and entities inside their interest area (grid cells around them). Nothing goes to all peers except rare global events (clock, server notices).
4 Every system has a time budget per frame and a queue; heavy work is spread across frames or moved off the main thread; when over budget the server sheds low-priority work (prefetch, cosmetic updates) first, never the world state.
5 Scale out by space: separate server processes per region (tile or sub-tile), with a gateway for login and a shared state store; a player belongs to one region process at a time.
6 Prove it with bots at 20/100/300/1000 and telemetry that fails the build when budgets are exceeded.

## 2 What the client may do and what the server must own
| Concern | Client may | Server must |
|---|---|---|
| Movement | move and step-up locally (as now), send position at 5-10 Hz | check speed (max about 1.5x run speed), reject teleports, own the position of record; clamp bounds, no flying for non-admins |
| Dig and fill | apply the visual hole at once (already done: 'applied a 0.9 m dig'), skip sending when the ground there is already open, hold-click limited to the tool animation rate | validate range/tool/stamina, charge a token, apply once, tell only the interest area; coalesce repeated digs of the same spot (592 fix) |
| Chop and gather | play the swing, show a hit effect, predict a drop sound | validate reach, tool, tree state, rate; own the tree HP, drops and loot rolls (luck.gd) |
| Crafting | show queue timers, pre-check ingredients | own the queue and completion time; one batched update per change |
| Inventory | local UI, optimistic move of stacks with rollback | own contents; accept compact actions (slot ids), send deltas not full inventory |
| World items, flora, NPCs, animals | interpolate, animate, cull by distance, cache blocks and flora on disk | decide state; send rows only inside interest, signature-diffed, unreliable for transforms |
| Water look | draw from received levels | simulate only tiles with players near; send level maps to subscribers only |
| Blocks and ground | disk cache with checksum and version, starter cache, prefetch with budget | generate on demand with a worker pool and a cap per client (cost-charged), serve from store; rate limit request_blocks by blocks per second |
| Chat/trade/economy | UI only | own, rate-limit, log |
| Voice (live NPC) | synthesise locally (Report 19) | send text only |
Rule of thumb: if a wrong value can give a player something (items, XP, position advantage), the server owns it; if it only changes what one screen looks like, the client owns it.

## 3 Rate limits: a token bucket per peer per action (one shared module, data in data/limits.json)
Mechanism: each peer has buckets {tokens, last_refill}; an action costs `cost` tokens (default 1; heavy actions cost more: charge by estimated server milliseconds); the bucket refills at `rate` per second up to `burst`. If tokens are short the request is dropped without reply (or a cheap reject for UI), a per-peer drop counter and a per-action counter go to telemetry. Escalation: more than 200 drops in 10 s -> warning to the player and log; 1000 in 60 s -> kick; repeat -> ban list by key. Also a global per-peer byte cap in and out. Start values (est., tune with bots and real play):
| Action (RPC) | rate/s | burst | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| report_position | 10 | 20 | extras dropped, keep latest |
| dig / fill | 3 | 6 | swing time 0.55 s gives about 2/s honestly |
| cut_flora / chop hit | 3 | 6 | per tool animation |
| inventory_action (generic) | 10 | 30 | craft start 2/s, place 2/s, trade 3/s via sub-costs |
| npc_talk / trade | 3 | 6 | |
| request_blocks / request_flora / request_water | 40 blocks, 8 chunks, 3 chunks | 80, 16, 6 | cost per item requested; this is the loading budget; a flyer at 40 m/s needs the highest |
| hello / reconnect | 0.2 | 3 | login storm control |
| admin_* | 5 | 10 | admin only, already gated |
Global admission: at most 5 new joins per second per process and a queue beyond that (the join stall is 1-3 s per client today; spread it or kill it). Load shedding: if the frame EMA exceeds the budget, cut in this order: prefetch (request_blocks urgent=0), NPC/animal send rate from 4 Hz to 1 Hz, far-entity updates, cosmetic RPCs; never drop inventory or damage.

## 4 Interest management (area of interest) by grid cell
Current state: only world items (NEAR_M grid in _sync_world_items), NPC and animal rows (rows_near) and trails are interest-filtered. Everything in section 0 item 3 is not. Player-to-player replication does not exist yet (no RPC carries other players), so it can be designed correctly from the start.
Design: a server-side grid of 64 m cells (one dictionary cell -> peer set, updated when a peer crosses a cell boundary). Event types carry a position; `emit(event, pos)` sends rpc_id to peers in cells within the event's radius (default 192 m, i.e. the 9-25 surrounding cells). Rules per event:
- dug / filled / flora_cut / world item changes: to peers in range only. Remove tile_changed per dig entirely: clients already apply the dig locally, and late loaders read the saved edit from the block store; keep tile_changed only for rebake or ground reset (rare), and make revisions per 256 m region not per tile (15,360 m tile) so one edit cannot invalidate a whole tile's prefetch.
- water_changed and water_rate: only to peers whose loaded tiles include the changed tile, and at most every 2 s per tile (the 592 client throttle of 3 s is a stopgap).
- Other players: send only the nearest 30-50 at full rate (10 Hz, delta-compressed, unreliable), the next 150 at 2 Hz, none beyond 150 m (bodies culled; names optional). Bandwidth bound per client: 50 x 24 B x 10 Hz = 12 KB/s (est.), 1000 clients = 12 MB/s total, fine.
- Hysteresis on enter/exit (leave radius larger than enter radius) so crossing a boundary does not flap.
- Every send path asks the grid, never loops all peers.
Order of work: add the grid and `emit` first; convert the six broadcast sites; then add player replication behind it.

## 5 Server frame budgeting
- A scheduler in terrain_server._process gives each system a budget per frame (microseconds) and a round-robin cursor: e.g. vitals 3 ms (a slice of players each frame: tick each peer once per second, staggered, not all each 0.25 s), npcs 2 ms, animals 2 ms, items 3 ms, trails 1 ms, send 4 ms, water apply 2 ms, RPC handling 8 ms, slack 25 ms. Each system processes work items until its budget is spent and resumes next frame (the existing telemetry marks give the instrumentation points).
- Heavy things move off the main thread: water simulation and its update application (poll is 250 ms per frame today, Sonny), saving (take a snapshot in the main thread cheaply, serialise and write in a worker; today items.save builds all inventories' JSON on the main thread), block generation (already a worker pool; cap queue depth and charge per client), welcome/inventory build.
- Dirty-flag everything: send inventory deltas, not full inventories; save only changed players; sync only changed items.
- Message costs: measure the cost of one RPC dispatch and one send in GDScript (step 0), because that sets how many messages per second the main thread can afford (guess: 20-50 microseconds each, so 1000 players at 5-10 Hz position reports alone are 5,000-10,000 messages/s = 0.1-0.5 s of a core per second; batch positions per peer per 100 ms into one packed message instead).
- Watchdog: the existing watchdog logs the stuck step; add an automatic action: if one frame exceeds 500 ms, dump the per-step times and drop the load-shedding level.
- GDScript limit: if after all this a single process still cannot hold about 100-150 active players (est.), move the hottest loops (vitals, grid, signatures) to a native module (GDExtension in C++ or Rust) or to threads; do not rewrite the server to another language before measuring.

## 6 Sharding and multiple processes (scale out)
- World is 9 tiles of 15,360 m (more later). Natural shard unit: a region process owns one tile (or one 2x2 block of sub-tiles of a busy tile) plus a halo of 200 m of read-only neighbour state for seamless view across borders.
- Processes: (1) gateway/login: authentication (identity provider, Steam id later), session tokens, queue, picks the region and hands the client an address and a ticket; (2) region servers: simulate their space (items, NPCs, animals, water for their tiles, ground edits), each its own ENet host with its own max clients (150-200); (3) world services: clock/weather, trade/market, chat, persistence, admin; talk to regions over a local link (ENet or TCP) with small messages.
- Persistence: per-player state out of per-process JSON files into a store with transactions (SQLite single-writer to start, Postgres later); player record loaded on entry to a region and written on exit and on a timer by a worker. Prevents two regions holding one player.
- Handoff: when a player crosses a tile border the region sends the player record to the neighbour, the client reconnects (or the gateway proxies); keep the player's old connection alive until the new one is ready (grace 5 s).
- Hotspots: the starter tile will hold most players. Use layering: N copies of the starter area, each capped (about 150 players), grouping by party/guild/friends; world items and NPCs per layer; ground edits shared (replayed) so the world feels the same.
- Order: do NOT shard first. Step 1-3 below (limits, interest, budgets) get one process to about 100-200 active players (est.); sharding is for beyond that.

## 7 Load test method
Bots: a light, protocol-only client (headless SceneTree script, no terrain, no rendering): connect with ENet, send hello, then a behaviour loop that sends the same RPCs a real client does (report_position random walk at 5 Hz, request_blocks around its position with a small fake cache to avoid re-requests, inventory_action for pick/craft, dig and chop at honest rates), and accepts and discards replies while counting bytes and measuring RTT with a ping action. One headless Godot process can run about 100-200 such bots (est.); 1000 bots = 5-10 processes on a separate 8-16 vCPU machine so the bots do not steal the server's CPU; use `tc netem` for 30-80 ms latency and 1 percent loss on a second run. Existing seeds: client flag --dig-spam=N (terrain_client.gd:488), tools/hole_stress.sh, src/worldgen/tools/stress_items_players.gd (20 simulated players). Extend those into one `tools/bots/run_bots.sh N behaviour`.
Behaviours: walker (positions only), loader (a flyer: 40 m/s, many blocks), digger (3 digs/s at one spot: the 592 case), chopper, crafter, trader, mixed (default), spammer (100 msgs/s of everything: must be contained by the limits), login storm (200 joins in 10 s), churn (join/leave every 30 s).
Ramps: 20 -> 100 -> 300 -> 1000, 10 minutes at each, then a 2 hour soak at the highest passing level. Every step records the telemetry below to CSV so runs compare.
Acceptance per level (pass/fail, the numbers go in tech/):
- server frame time p50 under 15 ms, p99 under 50 ms (20 Hz), no stall over 200 ms; no `WATCHDOG`;
- RPC handling plus broadcast under 8 ms/frame; outbound under 25 KB/s per client at steady state, under 150 KB/s while loading; inbound under 3 KB/s per client;
- bot RTT p95 under 150 ms (client perceived), zero disconnects not asked for, drop rate of honest actions under 1 percent;
- spammer bots lose at least 95 percent of their excess and do not change anyone else's p99;
- memory flat within 5 percent over the soak; save never stalls a frame over 50 ms.

## 8 Telemetry needed to prove it (on top of the 590/591 files)
- Per RPC name: calls, total microseconds, bytes in/out, drops by limiter. A table printed every 10 s and in the telemetry line (top 5 by time).
- Per system per frame: microseconds used vs budget, queue depth, items deferred; load-shed level.
- Per peer: bytes in/out per second, rtt, loss, drop counts, current rate bucket levels (admin view).
- Frame time histogram (p50/p95/p99/max per 10 s); GC/memory per 10 s; peers count by region and cell; players per cell max (hotspot detection).
- A single "capacity" line: players now, p99 frame ms, estimated headroom (players until budget burn 100 percent).
- Bot side: RTT p50/p95/p99, errors, reconnects.
Telemetry output stays small and goes to the same crash_reports/ files Dave already pushes; the bot runs write CSV under tech/logs/.

## 10 Risks and unknowns (what I could not verify)
- No measured cost per RPC, per vitals tick or per send: every "est." above is a guess to be replaced in step 0.
- Whether ENet is the bottleneck at hundreds of peers on one host (ENet itself handles thousands; Godot's multiplayer layer adds per-message overhead) is unmeasured.
- Godot's SceneMultiplayer `.rpc()` to all peers builds one packet per peer; there is no multicast, so interest filtering must be our own.
- Single-precision world coordinates (Dave decided: no double) is a client rendering issue and not a server scale one, but region origins help with it.
- The server is currently started by the client as a child process (listen-style) for single player; a real deployment needs the dedicated server path (scenes/server/server_main.gd) kept in sync and a way to run many region processes; confirm with Steve.
- Anti-cheat beyond sanity checks (memory editing etc.) is out of scope.

## 11b Who handles what: server or client, per feature (the centre of the plan; "cost at 1000" is per second across the whole world, est.)
Legend: S = server owns (decides, stores, validates), C = client may do (predict, cache, display). Rule used everywhere: if a wrong value can give a player an advantage (items, XP, position, damage) the server owns it; if it only changes what one screen shows, the client owns it. Other games: Gambetta and CS161 for the principle; Valheim as the counter-example (host-authority) and Minecraft (server authoritative blocks and movement checks).
| Feature | Owner | Why | Client may predict / cache | Cheat or stall risk | Cost at 1000 players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

# 12 Threading (Dave 2026-10-22: "we really want to take advantage of multi threading where we can")
## 12.1 Rules from the Godot docs (read from https://raw.githubusercontent.com/godotengine/godot-docs/master/tutorials/performance/thread_safe_apis.rst and the WorkerThreadPool class doc)
- Interacting with the ACTIVE SCENE TREE from a thread is not thread-safe; use call_deferred / set_deferred, or build node trees off-tree and add them on the main thread. Rendering nodes, physics (unless run-on-separate-thread is enabled) are not thread-safe. Singletons and servers are mostly thread-safe.
- GDScript Array and Dictionary: reading and writing EXISTING elements from several threads is acceptable; resizing, adding or removing needs a Mutex. Modifying one Resource from several threads is unsupported; do not load the same resource from two threads at once. AStar-style objects are not thread-safe (one thread per object).
- WorkerThreadPool: add_task (low priority by default, high_priority flag), add_group_task(callable, elements) which runs once per index over all workers (the easy data-parallel tool); every task must be waited on with wait_for_task_completion so its resources are freed; waiting from inside a task can return ERR_BUSY (deadlock guard); the doc warns the pool "may hurt performance if the distributed work isn't computationally expensive". Pool size/low-priority ratio settings exist in project settings (threading/worker_pool/max_threads and low_priority_thread_ratio from memory: verify in 4.7.2 before relying on them).
- Our own history: a worker-thread crash (build 204, "owner thread bug": about 1 start in 10-20 a worker died when several made their first flora chunk at once, terrain_server.gd comment near line 412) shows the real risk: first-use initialisation of shared objects. Rule: initialise shared things on the main thread before workers start; one writer per container; hand results over by a mutex-guarded queue; no scene-tree access in workers.

## 12.3 A likely cheap giant win (unmeasured, found by reading): _poll_water counts wet cells by looping `for level in update[1]: if not is_nan(level)` in GDScript on the main thread for every water update (terrain_server.gd, about lines 801-806). A tile is 15,360 m at an 8 m grid = 1,920 x 1,920 = 3.69 million floats; at 50-80 ns per GDScript iteration that is about 180-300 ms per update, which matches Willy's 250 ms per frame. Do the counting once in the water thread (or with a packed-array method) and hand the main thread only the count and the array reference. Expected: poll cost from about 250 ms to under 2 ms. Sonny owns water; verify by timing that loop first.
## 12.4 Recommended thread model per system, ordered by payoff
The pattern that makes the rest possible: MAIN thread = network I/O and tiny work only (receive RPC, validate with a token bucket, push a command into a per-system queue, apply finished results, send). WORKERS = simulation partitions that own their data (no shared writes), reading a snapshot or owning a cell/peer range, writing results to an outbox applied by main. Godot's multiplayer layer is main-thread only, so sending stays on main (or on dedicated sockets like the block line).
| Rank | System | Thread model | Why / how | Risk |

## 12.5 Rules to keep it safe (put in the code review checklist)
1 One writer per container; mutex around any resize; hand results by queue; no node access in workers; initialise shared singletons before starting workers (build-204 crash).
2 Every WorkerThreadPool task is waited or its completion polled and disposed.
3 Telemetry per thread: busy percent of each pool, queue depth, task latency p95; fail the bot test if the main thread waits on a lock for more than 2 ms.
4 Test with the existing --threads=N flag at 1, 2, 4, 11 to find races (a race often shows at 1 thread vs 11), and with Godot's thread sanitizer builds if available in our custom build.
5 Prefer data-parallel group tasks over many tiny tasks (the doc warns tiny tasks lose to overhead): chunk work to at least 0.3 ms per task.

# 13 Load-adaptive exertion (Dave 2026-10-22; Tyler_002 design note for Steve_002; NO CODE until approved)
Dave's idea: no pointless dig counting. When the server is overloaded, every action that adds server load gradually costs more exertion and takes longer. It is invisible: players tire sooner, stop and rest, and that relieves the load. "The weaker the signal (the 5 bars), the harder the exertion." Full bars = no change.

## 13.1 One load level, shared by the bars and the exertion
- The SERVER measures its own load and publishes one integer, the load level L from 0 (healthy) to 4 (overloaded). It rides in the existing 4 Hz `pulse` heartbeat (one byte; protocol bump with the standing approval).
- The client shows bars = min(its own link bars from rtt/loss/silence, 5 - L). So "weaker signal" really means "server is strained" and the bars tell the truth about it (a green-bars-but-nothing-works case, as Dave saw at 02:36, cannot hide any more).
- The exertion multiplier uses ONLY L (server load), never the client's own link bars. A player on poor wifi is not punished for it and it would not relieve the server anyway. See the question for Dave below.

## 13.2 How L is measured (server side, smoothed)
Inputs, each sampled once a second: main-thread frame time EMA against its budget (budget = 25 ms of a 50 ms frame, SCALE_PLAN section 5), worst gap in the last 5 s, and queue depths (flora/bury worker, block queue, pending RPCs). Score s = max(frame_EMA / budget, gap_5s / 200 ms, queue_depth / queue_limit) so the worst signal wins.
| Score s | Level L | Bars shown (server part) | Exertion and time multiplier m |
|---|---|---|---|
| under 0.5 | 0 | 5 | 1.00 (no change) |
| 0.5 to 0.7 | 1 | 4 | 1.15 |
| 0.7 to 0.9 | 2 | 3 | 1.35 |
| 0.9 to 1.2 | 3 | 2 | 1.70 |
| over 1.2 | 4 | 1 | 2.20 (hard cap 2.5) |
(Numbers are starting values to be tuned with the bot ladder, step 0 of the plan.)

## 13.3 Avoiding oscillation and unfairness
- Hysteresis: L rises one step only after s stays above that step's threshold for 3 s; it falls one step only after s stays 10 percent below the step's lower threshold for 10 s. It moves at most one step per change, so m ramps like a slope, not a switch.
- Slow loop: exertion drains over tens of seconds, so the feedback is slow by nature; the smoothing above is slower than that loop on the way up and much slower on the way down, which prevents a limit cycle of "everyone rests, load drops, everyone works, load spikes".
- Everyone gets the same m at the same time (one level per server process), so nobody is singled out. Who really causes the load is handled by the per-peer token bucket (below), not by this.
- A cap of 2.5 and a floor of 1.0 keep the game playable; L never exceeds 4 even if the server is on fire (then the bars show 1 and the hard load shedding of section 5 takes over).

## 13.4 Which actions scale
Scaled by m (cost in exertion AND duration/cooldown): dig and fill (DIG_COOLDOWN_MS, exertion per swing), chop and mining hits, gather and pickup pace, place structure, craft duration, trade pace, fishing casts. Not scaled: movement and fall, eating, drinking, sleeping, fainting, anything that keeps a player alive, talking to NPCs, opening the inventory, moving stacks, drops on death, the heartbeat itself, and every admin action. Exertion cost uses the existing per-action numbers (items/BALANCE.md) times m; time uses the existing cooldowns times m. All of it is a single function `Load.scale(kind)` in one shared module so no action decides on its own.

## 13.5 Relation to the token-bucket limiter and the bars
- Token bucket (src/shared/action_limits.gd): per peer, fast and hard, stops a script or a stuck click; costs the honest player nothing. Exertion scaling: collective, slow and soft, makes honest play lighter when the whole server is strained. They never overlap: the bucket still drops floods even at L 0.
- Bars: same L as above. The server never lies to the player about the bars to get the effect: invisible means "no message about exertion", not "hidden signal".

## 13.6 Host-and-play and small groups are never throttled
L is held at 0 whenever 24 or fewer players are connected, whatever the load number says (the measurement still runs and is logged for telemetry). Steve's condition: a friend session of 10 to 20 on an 8 GB machine never feels this. On a dedicated server the 24 is a config value (data/limits.json `load_free_players`).

## 13.7 Telemetry and tests
Telemetry line gets `load L, s, m, peers`. Headless test: bots at 20 / 100 / 300 with spade digs; assert L stays 0 at 20, rises monotonically with load, never exceeds the cap, and returns to 0 within 30 s of the load ending; assert dig cooldown and exertion are multiplied exactly by m. Needs SCALE_PLAN step 0 (the protocol bots) first; the frame-time and queue measures are tiny additions to terrain_server._process.

## 13.8 Honest limits
It only helps if players actually do the load-adding work with their hands; bots and spammers are handled by the token bucket and kicks. It cannot fix a stall caused by one expensive action (like the 6 s plant check): those are bugs to remove, and the level would only mask them. It is a safety valve for crowd load, not a substitute for the worker-thread work.
