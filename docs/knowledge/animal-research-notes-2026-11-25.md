# Animal research notes: birds, shore, mountain, plains, big game, domestic (25 Nov 2026)

Research notes behind `docs/design/ANIMAL_SCENARIOS.md`. Raw notes, not rules. Sources marked (V) were opened and checked; the rest are standard values to spot-check. Lines marked as not from a source are general knowledge.

## How to read this
- **Citations:** each bullet ends with a tag like `[S12]`. The full URLs are in the source list at the end. I used tags because 40+ species with full URLs on every bullet would be unreadable.
- **Checked (V):** I opened and checked 16 sources against the figures quoted from them. They are marked **(V)** in the list.
- **Not checked:** every other source came up in searches by title only. The figures cited to them are standard textbook or literature values for the topic, not checked against the page. Spot-check those before treating them as fixed. A `~` marks a typical or approximate value.
- **No research done:** for these the bullets are my own knowledge, not sourced facts: a few stock values (horse, donkey, domestic cow and goat speeds), the elephant musth details, and the honey badger speed, bark and night travel figures.
- **Source quality:** wildlife agencies, extension services, zoo fact sheets and peer-reviewed papers wherever possible. I used no Wikipedia pages.
- **Fire versus the design rule (Dave's "fire keeps everything away from the players"):**
  - In reality fire is a strong repellent for most wild animals, which detect it by smoke smell, crackle and heat and mostly flee [S67] [S68].
  - It is not absolute in the wild. Habituated or food-conditioned animals (hyenas, stray dogs, crows, gulls, elephants at crops) do approach occupied camps. Animals at savanna waterholes fled human voices about twice as readily as lion sounds [S6].
  - The design rule overrides the real behaviour. Where a species below says it ignores fire in reality, treat that as flavour only: animals can circle the edge of the firelight but never come in.

## BIRDS

### Songbirds (generic passerines)
- **Speed:** flight ~25–40 km/h, in short bursts from cover to cover. On the ground they hop or walk slowly. [S62]
- **Senses:** sight is dominant, with wide monocular fields. Hearing is used for alarm calls. Smell is negligible. [S62]
- **Flight distance:** small passerines typically flush at ~5–20 m from a walking person. Larger-bodied birds flush farther. Urban birds tolerate much closer approach than rural birds. [S62]
- **Activity:** daytime only, with a dawn chorus peak and a dusk calling peak. They roost at night in dense shrubs or cavities and fall silent. [S62]
- **Groups:** pairs or territorial singles in the breeding season. Mixed flocks of 5–50+ in winter. [S62]
- **Threat response:** a high thin "seet" call for a hawk overhead, which makes birds freeze or dive into cover. Harsh "chip" or "chatter" calls are for mobbing a ground predator, owl, cat or snake. Mobbing can draw several species together. [S59]
- **Cornered or caught:** they struggle and give a distress call. They do not attack people.
- **Nests:** some species do a distraction display. Mobbing calls intensify near the nest.
- **Weather:** they shelter and go quiet in rain and wind. They fluff up in cold and flock to food. Song resumes right after a storm.
- **Fire:** they flee smoke, and some species forage at burned edges afterwards. [S67]

### American crow / carrion crow
- **Speed:** flight ~40–50 km/h. They walk on the ground. [S49]
- **Senses:** excellent sight. Crows recognise individual human faces, remember a "dangerous" face for years, and teach other crows to scold it. [S48]
- **Flight distance:** wary of people who look at them or carry long objects. Typical flush is ~15–50 m rural and much less urban. [S48]
- **Activity:** daytime. In winter they gather into communal roosts at dusk; American crow roosts can hold thousands. [S49]
- **Groups:** carrion crows are territorial pairs, with non-breeding flocks. American crows live in family groups whose helpers stay on from earlier broods. [S49]
- **Sounds:** a harsh "caw" or "kraa" in repeated series. Rapid, loud cawing is the mobbing or alarm call, used against owls, hawks, cats and threatening humans. It is frequent and draws in other crows. [S48] [S49]
- **Threat response:** mobbing; they dive-bomb hawks and owls and rarely people. Crows near nests may swoop at people.
- **Scavenging:** opportunistic. They follow predators and people to carcasses and camps. In reality they approach camps after people leave.
- **Weather:** active in most weather. They roost early in storms.

### Ring-necked pheasant
- **Escape:** prefers to run into cover rather than fly. When pressed it bursts into flight at close range (often 2–10 m), with loud wing whirr and a cackle. Flight speed is ~60 km/h but it covers only a few hundred metres before gliding down. [S50]
- **Senses:** keen sight and hearing. [S50]
- **Activity:** feeds at dawn and late afternoon, often along field edges. Roosts at night on the ground in grass or cattails, or in trees. [S50]
- **Groups:** winter flocks segregated by sex. In spring a cock holds a crowing territory with a harem of hens. [S50]
- **Sounds:**
  - Cocks give a loud 2-note "kork-kok" crow followed by wing drumming, every few minutes at dawn in spring. [S50]
  - On flushing they give a "kuk-kuk-kuk" cackle.
  - Thunder or blasts can set off crowing.
- **Nests:** hens sit tight on the nest and flush only at the last moment.
- **Weather:** blizzards drive them into dense cover (cattails, shelterbelts), and severe winters cause heavy mortality. [S50]

### Ruffed grouse / red grouse
- **Ruffed grouse escape:** sits tight relying on camouflage, then explodes into flight at ~3–10 m and puts trees between itself and the threat. [S51]
- **Ruffed grouse drumming:** males stand on a log and beat their wings to make a low, accelerating thump. It is heard up to ~400 m, mostly at dawn in spring and also in autumn. [S51]
- **Ruffed grouse in winter:** plunges into deep snow to roost, which insulates it from the cold. [S51]
- **Groups (ruffed):** mostly solitary. Hens have a brood of ~8–12 chicks. [S51]
- **Ruffed grouse hens:** a hen with chicks does a broken-wing or hissing distraction display and may even rush at intruders. [S51]
- **Red grouse:** lives on heather moorland in pairs or family coveys, which pack into larger flocks in autumn and winter. [S52]
- **Red grouse call:** the famous "go-back go-back-back" is given on flushing and in territorial display flights, especially at dawn. [S52]
- **Red grouse escape:** flies low and fast, whirring then gliding, and drops back into heather a few hundred metres away. [S52]
- **Predators:** fox, goshawk, owls, hen harrier and golden eagle. Raptors ambush with short, fast attacks. [S51] [S52]
- **Weather:** snow and cold rain hit chick survival hardest. [S52]

### Mallard
- **Speed:** flight ~65–90 km/h, and it takes off vertically straight from the water. It waddles slowly on land. [S53]
- **Senses:** sharp sight. [S53]
- **Flight distance:** wild birds flush at ~20–100 m. Park birds approach people for food. [S53]
- **Activity:** feeds day and dusk, and some feed at night. Sleeps on the water, often with one eye open, or on banks. [S53]
- **Groups:** pairs in spring. Flocks of tens to hundreds in autumn and winter. [S53]
- **Sounds:** only females give the loud "quack" series, which serves as alarm, contact and the decrescendo call. Males give a soft raspy "rhaeb". Alarm quacks are given on takeoff. [S53]
- **With young:** the hen does a broken-wing distraction display to lead predators away from the ducklings, which scatter or dive. [S53]
- **Moult:** flightless for ~3–4 weeks in late summer and hides in reeds. [S53]
- **Weather:** stays wherever open water remains, and freezing pushes it south.

### Canada goose / greylag goose
- **Pairs:** mate for life. [S54]
- **Nest defence:** the gander guards while the goose incubates for 28 days. A defending goose hisses, spreads its wings, then runs or flies at a person and strikes with wing and bill. [S54]
- **Habituation:** suburban or habituated geese attack more readily than rural ones. Rural geese tend to flee. [S54]
- **Moult:** flightless from mid-June to early July (adults and goslings), and they escape to water. [S54]
- **Groups:** family groups. Autumn and winter flocks of hundreds to thousands, flying in V-formation. [S55] [S56]
- **Sounds:** constant loud honking in flight and on alarm. Hissing is a close-range threat. [S55] [S56]
- **Activity:** daytime grazing, and they roost on water at night for safety. [S55]
- **Flight:** ~50–65 km/h, faster with a tailwind on migration. [S55]
- **Greylag:** the European equivalent with similar behaviour. Its call is a deep "aahng-ung-ung", like a farmyard goose; it is the ancestor of domestic geese. [S56]
- **Weather:** frozen water forces them to move.

### Herring gull (seabird)
- **Speed:** flies at ~40–50 km/h and soars on wind. It walks well on land. [S58]
- **Senses:** sharp sight. They notice food from far off and follow other gulls diving on food. [S57]
- **Groups:** nests in colonies from tens to thousands. Flocks loaf on beaches and gather at dumps. [S58]
- **Colony defence:** near nests they give an alarm "ha-ha-ha" (a "kek" series), make dive-bomb passes at people's heads, strike with feet, and defecate or vomit on intruders. Defence peaks while chicks are present. [S57] [S58]
- **Sounds:** the "long call" is a loud laughing series for display, given many times a day in colonies. A mew call is used for chicks. [S57]
- **Activity:** mostly daytime, and they roost on water or islands at night. [S58]
- **Around people:** a scavenger. Bold at camps and beaches, it steals food and approaches to within a few metres. [S58]
- **Weather:** shelters inland in storms and rides strong wind easily.

### Domestic chicken
- **Speed:** runs at ~14 km/h. Short flutter-flights of a few metres. [S59]
- **Alarm calls are referential:** [S59]
  - An aerial predator (hawk) triggers a soft high-pitched "eeee". Hens crouch, freeze and look up.
  - A ground predator (fox, dog) triggers loud, repeated "cut-cut-cut-CAW" cackling. The flock stands tall and scans or runs.
- **Food call:** roosters give "tuk-tuk" to call hens to food. [S59]
- **Activity:** daytime. They go to roost at dusk on perches and are near-helpless in darkness. [S59]
- **Groups:** one rooster with several hens; the pecking order is well established. [S59]
- **Defence:** hens with chicks puff up, scream and peck at intruders, and chicks run under the hen. Roosters spur-attack people and dogs. [S59]
- **Crowing:** roosters crow at dawn and through the day, often dozens of times. [S59]
- **Weather:** heat causes panting, and they huddle in cold and rain.

### Helmeted guineafowl
- **Escape:** runs fast, preferring to run rather than fly. Strong short flights of up to a few hundred metres into trees. [S60]
- **Senses:** very alert and noisy, and acts as a sentinel species. [S60]
- **Groups:** flocks of ~10–25, and over 50 outside the breeding season. They split into pairs to breed. [S60] [S61]
- **Activity:** daytime. They roost communally in tall trees at night. [S60]
- **Alarm call:** a loud, harsh, repeated "kek-kek-kek-kaaaa" chatter, given at the first sign of any predator or person. It carries a long way and other animals heed it. [S60]
- **Mobbing:** a flock will mob and even attack snakes and small predators. [S60]
- **Flight distance:** wild flocks walk or run off ahead of people at ~30–100 m. [S60]
- **Weather:** thermoregulates by behaviour, shading at midday and sunning in the morning. [S60]

## WATER / SHORE

### Harbour seal
- **Speed:** swims ~15–20 km/h in bursts. On land it is very slow and clumsy, moving by humping.
- **Senses:** excellent underwater hearing and whisker touch. Good underwater vision. On land it relies on sight and sound. [S34]
- **Disturbance from boats:** close approaches (~100 m) flush almost the whole haul-out; ~89% flushed at ≤100 m from large ships. Ships begin flushing seals at ~400 m and at up to ~1 km. [S34] (V)
- **Disturbance from people on foot:** they flush from beach walkers and kayaks at shorter ranges, and nursing is interrupted. [S35]
- **Activity:** hauls out on rocks and sandbars at low tide and in daytime to rest. Forages mostly at night and in the water. [S34]
- **Groups:** loose haul-out groups of a few to hundreds. They are not tightly social. [S35]
- **Threat response:** first sign is the head raised. Then they move toward the water and stampede into the sea. [S34]
- **Cornered or caught:** on land they bite hard. They are rarely aggressive otherwise.
- **With young:** pups are left on the beach while the mother forages, and are vulnerable. [S35]
- **Sounds:** mostly quiet. Growls and snorts at close quarters. Males make underwater roars in the breeding season.
- **Predators:** orcas and sharks hunt at sea, polar bears in ice regions, and humans on beaches.
- **Weather:** stays in the water in storms and hauls out more in sunny, calm weather.

### Frog: common frog / American bullfrog
- **Escape:** jumps into water at the approach of a person. Flush distance is ~1–3 m for common frogs and further for wary bullfrogs. [S38] [S40]
- **Jumping:** bullfrogs leap up to ~1–2 m. [S39]
- **Senses:** motion-sensitive vision; they ignore still objects. Good hearing for calls. [S39]
- **Activity:** mostly at dusk and night, especially in warm, wet weather. They hide by day. [S38] [S40]
- **Common frog calls:** a soft purring croak at spawning ponds in early spring, with mass gatherings over a few nights. [S40]
- **Bullfrog calls:** males give a deep "jug-o-rum" from June to July on warm nights. It is heard up to ~1.6 km (about a mile), repeated many times, and males hold calling territories. [S38] [S39]
- **When caught:** they struggle and may give a loud distress scream with the mouth open. Big bullfrogs bite.
- **Diet:** bullfrogs are voracious and eat anything that fits, including birds and snakes. [S38]
- **Predators:** herons, snakes, otters, raccoons and fish.
- **Weather:**
  - Rain triggers movement and calling.
  - Cold leads to hibernation: common frogs in pond mud or leaf litter, bullfrogs in pond mud. [S38] [S40]
  - Drought sends them into hiding.

### American alligator
- **Speed:** short land bursts of ~15–18 km/h over a few metres; it cannot sustain a chase. Swims ~30 km/h in bursts. [S37]
- **Senses:**
  - Eyes, ears and nostrils sit atop the head while submerged.
  - Pressure sensors in the jaw detect ripples.
  - Good night vision. [S37]
- **Temperature:** most active at 28–33 °C. Stops feeding below ~21 °C and goes dormant below ~13 °C, overwintering in dens or "gator holes". [S36] (V)
- **Activity:** basks by day. Hunts mostly at dusk and night by ambush at the water's edge. [S36]
- **Near people:** usually retreats into the water. Alligators fed by people lose their fear and approach. UF and FWC advise keeping a distance of more than ~10 m and staying away from the water's edge at dusk. [S37]
- **Close quarters:** opens the mouth and gives a loud hiss, then makes a lunge or sideways snap. Attacks are a fast lunge from the water followed by a death roll. [S37]
- **Courtship:** in April–June, males and females make deep bellows, especially at dawn, often in group choruses. Males make infrasonic "water dance" vibrations. [S36] [S37]
- **Nest defence:** females guard mound nests for the ~63–68-day incubation from June to September. They aggressively defend nests and pods of young, sometimes for a year or more. Hatchlings give a high "umph" grunt to call the mother. [S36]
- **Social:** cannibalism and fighting among alligators are major causes of adult death. [S36]

## MOUNTAIN

### Bighorn sheep
- **Speed:** ~50–55 km/h on flat ground and agile on cliffs. Lambs outrun their mothers by one month old. [S41] [S42]
- **Senses:** keen eyesight is the primary alarm sense and they spot movement far off. Good hearing and smell. [S42]
- **Groups:** ewes and lambs in groups of 10+. Rams form bachelor groups outside the rut. [S41]
- **Rut:** mid-October to early December, peaking in November–December. Rams clash horns head-on at ~30+ km/h with a crack audible over ~1 km, sometimes for hours. [S41] [S42]
- **Lambing:** early May. Ewes isolate on cliffs, and lambs hide under the mother against golden eagles. [S41]
- **Escape:** they flee toward and up rugged escape terrain and rarely stand and fight a predator. [S41]
- **Predators:** cougars ambush from cover near cliffs, plus wolves and coyotes on flat ground, and golden eagles take lambs.
- **Sounds:** mostly quiet. Lambs and ewes bleat; snorts and wheezes signal alarm.
- **Activity:** feeds by day, mostly morning and evening, and rests on ledges at midday and night. [S42]
- **Weather:** in deep snow they move to windswept slopes. Wet, cold springs raise lamb mortality.

### Mountain goat
- **Movement:** cautious, very sure-footed climber. Not a fast runner (~30 km/h short bursts), and it escapes by climbing onto ledges predators cannot reach. [S44] [S45]
- **Senses:** good eyesight. [S44]
- **People:** salt-seeking goats approach hikers for urine and sweat. NPS advises keeping at least 50 yards (~45 m) away. If a goat follows, yell, wave a coat and throw rocks. [S43] (V)
- **Habituation:** habituated goats stand their ground and may assert dominance. A fatal goring occurred in Olympic National Park in 2010. [S43] [S44]
- **Rut:** October–December, peaking in November. Males become more aggressive and use their dagger horns to defend personal space. [S43]
- **Groups:** nannies with kids and yearlings in groups of ~2–10+. Billies are solitary or in small groups. [S45]
- **Kids:** born in May–June. The nanny stands below or beside the kid on cliffs and will butt even wolves. [S44]
- **Predators:** cougar, wolf, bear and golden eagle (which knocks kids off cliffs). Avalanches and falls are major killers. [S44]
- **Sounds:** mostly silent. Grunts, snorts and bleating by kids.
- **Weather:** very cold-tolerant with a thick coat. In heavy snow they stay on windblown ledges.

### Chamois (Alps)
- **Flight distance from hikers:** mean ~124 m (range 15–367 m). [S46] (V)
- **Alert distance:** ~176 m. Chamois first noticed people at ~400 m when the view was open. [S46] (V)
- **Response style:** running escapes were rare; most chamois walked off a few metres, and 73% of herds reacted only when the person left the trail. [S46] (V)
- **Dogs:** hunters' or hikers' dogs caused flights of over 500 m. [S46] (V)
- **Paragliders:** produce strong flight responses and can push chamois out of an area. [S47]
- **Groups:** 39% of herds were single animals and 31% had five or more, up to ~33. Herds included kids in 25% of cases. [S46] (V)
- **Escape:** flees uphill or across rock. It is very fast and agile on steep slopes (~50 km/h bursts).
- **Alarm:** a sharp whistling snort and foot-stamping, given at once on detecting a predator and repeated.
- **Rut:** November–December. Bucks chase each other in long high-speed pursuits. [S46]
- **Activity:** feeds at dawn and dusk and rests on ledges at midday, especially in summer heat.
- **Weather:** in winter it descends to forest. Avalanches and deep snow cause mortality.

## OPEN PLAINS

### Pronghorn
- **Speed:** up to ~88 km/h (55 mph). [S1] (V)
- **Endurance:** sustains ~65 km/h for several km and ~45 km/h for 10+ km. It has enormous aerobic capacity. [S3]
- **Senses:** vision is dominant, with ~320° field and detection of moving objects at ~5 km (3 mi). [S1] (V)
- **Habitat use:** rests and feeds in wide-open areas where it sees threats far off. [S1] (V)
- **Flight distance:** typically flees at ~100–400 m from people on foot. It flashes its white rump hair as an alarm signal to the herd. [S2]
- **Groups:** small summer bands and winter herds of up to hundreds. [S2]
- **Rut:** September–October. Bucks defend harems or territories, chase rivals and snort-wheeze. [S2]
- **Fawns:** born May–June, often twins. They hide motionless in grass for ~3 weeks. Fawn predation, mainly by coyotes, plus severe winters, are the main causes of death. [S1] [S2]
- **Predators:** coyotes ambush hidden fawns, and adults outrun everything. [S2]
- **Sounds:** a snort or blowing alarm, and bucks make a wheeze in the rut. Otherwise quiet.
- **Weather:** deep snow and fences trap them, and winter die-offs happen. [S1]

### Blue wildebeest
- **Speed:** ~80 km/h (50+ mph) when fleeing predators. [S18] (V)
- **Groups:** herds of 10–1,000 females and young plus bachelor groups. Territorial males are solitary. The Serengeti migration exceeds 1.8 million animals. [S18] (V)
- **Calving:** synchronised in February, with over 500,000 calves born in 2–3 weeks. Calves stand within ~6 minutes and run with the herd soon after, which swamps predators. [S18] (V)
- **Predators:** lions, spotted hyenas, leopards, wild dogs and cheetahs. [S18] (V)
- **Lion ambush:** lions kill wildebeest near cover, erosion gullies and water rather than on open plains. [S5] (V)
- **Hyena chase:** hyenas run them down in chases of up to a few km. [S7]
- **Alarm response:** the herd stops, stares, snorts, then bolts and wheels. Flight distance from people on foot is ~100–300 m.
- **Sounds:** a constant nasal "gnu" grunt, heard frequently especially in the migration and rut. Snorts signal alarm. [S18]
- **Rut:** territorial bulls bellow, paw and fight in short horn bouts. [S18]
- **Seasonal movement:** rainfall drives the migration toward green grass. River crossings cause mass drownings. [S18]

### Eastern grey kangaroo
- **Speed:** hops at ~50–60 km/h in bursts. Cruises at ~20–25 km/h for long distances. Slow pentapedal walk while grazing. [S20]
- **Activity:** mostly at dawn and dusk; they rest in shade during the day. [S19] (V)
- **Groups:** mobs of breeding females, young and several males, with one dominant male breeding. Typical mob size is ~2–10, gathering to 50+ on good pasture. [S19] (V) [S20]
- **Near people:** wild kangaroos thump the ground with a hind foot as an alarm and hop away. [S20]
- **Aggression risk:** very small, but animals fed by people may approach and turn aggressive. Females with joeys may defend them. Injured animals are dangerous. [S19] (V)
- **Warning signs:** arched back, chest-rubbing on grass, standing tall on the tail, growling or clucking, and sparring. [S19] (V)
- **Attacks:** grabs with the forearms and kicks with the hind claws while balancing on the tail. Large males can disembowel dogs. [S19]
- **Chased:** heads for water and may drown a pursuing dog. [S20]
- **Predators:** dingoes run kangaroos down in relays and chases. [S20]
- **Sounds:** foot-thump for alarm, coughs for submission, and clucking between mother and joey.
- **Weather:** drought brings mass movement to green feed. They rest in shade in heat.

### Feral / wild horse
- **Speed:** gallops ~55–70 km/h. Trots ~13–20 km/h for long periods; walks ~6 km/h. *(standard equine values, not checked against a source)*
- **Senses:** wide-angle vision (~350°) tuned to movement, acute hearing, and good smell.
- **Groups:** family bands of one stallion (sometimes 2–3) with several mares and foals, usually ~3–20. Lone males form bachelor bands. [S21] [S22]
- **Alarm response:** head up, ears forward and a loud snort. The band bunches and flees, with the lead mare at the front and the stallion often at the rear. [S21]
- **Flight distance:** wild bands typically move off at ~50–200 m from people on foot.
- **Cornered:** they rear, strike with the forefeet, and kick with the hind feet. Stallions bite.
- **Stallion fights:** stallions fight over mares with biting, rearing and kicking. They herd mares with a lowered-head "snaking" posture. [S21]
- **Foals:** foals follow the mare within hours of birth. Mares and stallions will kick at wolves, dogs and coyotes.
- **Rest:** they sleep standing up for short naps, and lie down for deep sleep only when others keep watch.
- **Sounds:** a whinny or neigh for contact, carrying ~1 km; a nicker greeting; a squeal in fights; a snort for alarm.
- **Weather:** in storms they turn their rumps to the wind, and they shelter in winter.

### Feral donkey (burro)
- **Speed:** gallops ~50 km/h in short bursts. A steady walker; very hardy and water-efficient.
- **Senses:** large ears and excellent hearing. [S23]
- **Groups:** loose groups. Some jacks hold territories near water. [S23]
- **Temperament:** less flighty than horses. When frightened a donkey often freezes and assesses rather than bolting; "stubbornness" is caution. [S23]
- **Hostility to canids:** strongly hostile to dogs, coyotes and wolves. Donkeys bray, chase, kick, strike, bite and stomp, which is why they are used as livestock guards. [S23] [S24]
- **Cornered:** kicks with front and hind feet and bites.
- **Jennies:** jennies with foals are protective. [S24]
- **Sounds:** the "hee-haw" bray carries ~3 km. It is used for contact, alarm and when separated, often at dawn and dusk. [S23]
- **Weather:** desert-adapted; seeks shade at midday. The coat is not waterproof, so donkeys shelter from rain.

### Dromedary camel
- **Speed:** up to ~65 km/h in sprints. Sustains ~40 km/h for an hour or so. Walks ~5 km/h all day. [S25]
- **Senses:** good sight and smell, and can detect water from a distance. [S25]
- **Groups:** a bull with several females and young; groups of ~2–20. [S25]
- **People:** domestic or feral camels tolerate people. Wary feral camels move off at ~100 m+.
- **Rut:** during the rut, which follows the rains and cooler months, bulls become dangerous. They gurgle, inflate the dulla (a pink palate sac pushed out of the mouth), froth, and spread poll-gland secretion. [S26]
- **Rut fighting:** rutting bulls neck-wrestle, bite and chase, and attack people. [S26]
- **Cornered:** bites hard, kicks in all directions, spits cud, and crushes by kneeling on a target.
- **With calf:** mothers are protective.
- **Sounds:** groans, moans and bellows when loaded or disturbed, and roaring gurgles in the rut. [S26]
- **Activity:** mostly daytime. In the midday heat they rest facing the sun to minimise exposed area. [S25]
- **Weather:** tolerates heat and sandstorms; it can close its nostrils. Cold and wet weather is a problem.

### Cape buffalo / wild cattle (aurochs-like)
- **Speed:** up to ~55 km/h. [S17]
- **Senses:** smell and hearing are good and eyesight is moderate. [S17]
- **Groups:** herds of 50 to 500+, sometimes 1,000+. Old bulls ("dagga boys") live alone or in small groups. [S17]
- **Activity:** grazes at night and in the cool hours. Rests and wallows in mud at midday. [S17]
- **People:** herds usually stare, then move off.
- **Danger to people:** lone bulls and wounded buffalo are extremely dangerous. Wounded animals are known to circle back and ambush hunters from cover. [S17]
- **Group defence:** buffalo mob lions, rescue herd-mates, and have killed lions. A calf's distress bleat brings the herd running. [S17]
- **Predators:** lions mostly take calves, old bulls and isolated animals. A buffalo hunt by lions can last hours.
- **Sounds:** grunts and croaks for herd movement signals, bellows, and a distress bawl.
- **Weather:** drought concentrates herds at water and makes them more dangerous there.
- **Chillingham wild cattle (UK):** a semi-wild herd without human handling, wary and potentially aggressive, especially when calving. The closest living model of aurochs behaviour. [S30]

### Domestic cow
- **Speed:** runs ~25–40 km/h in short bursts; walks ~4 km/h. *(standard livestock values, not checked against a source)*
- **Senses:** panoramic vision (~300°) with a blind spot directly behind, poor depth perception, and sensitive hearing. [S28] [S29]
- **Flight zone:** tame cattle have none. Range cattle have a large zone, and it grows when they are excited or approached head-on. [S27] (V)
- **Point of balance:** at the shoulder. A person behind it makes cattle move forward; in front, they back up. [S27] (V)
- **Calming down:** after excitement, cattle take 20–30 minutes to calm. [S27] (V)
- **Groups:** herd animals. Isolated cattle become agitated and dangerous. [S28]
- **Cows with new calves:** very protective. They charge people and dogs and head-butt and trample. [S28]
- **Bulls:** dairy bulls are the most dangerous. A lowered head, pawing and a side-on display mean a charge is coming. [S28]
- **Sounds:** mooing for contact and calf-calling, frequent when separated. Bellowing for distress or a bull's challenge. [S28]
- **Weather:** in heat they seek shade and water. In storms they turn their tails to the wind and drift.

### Goat (domestic / feral)
- **Speed:** runs ~25–30 km/h. An excellent climber. *(standard values, not checked against a source)*
- **Feeding:** a browser that eats shrubs. [S31] [S32]
- **Senses:** wide vision and good hearing and smell. [S31]
- **Groups:** herds with a dominance hierarchy. Feral groups range from ~5 to over 100. [S32] [S33]
- **Alarm:** a loud sneeze-snort and foot stamp. The herd bunches and flees, preferring high ground. [S31] [S33]
- **Kids:** "lying-out" type. Kids hide while the mother browses, and she returns and calls. [S31]
- **Rut:** autumn. Bucks stink, urinate on themselves, and butt-fight. [S31]
- **Cornered:** head-butts.
- **Sounds:** bleats and bawls; very vocal when separated or hungry. [S33]
- **Weather:** hates rain and shelters quickly, since goats lack a waterproof coat. [S32]

## AFRICA / ASIA BIG GAME

### Lion
- **Speed and stamina:** charges at ~50–60 km/h but tires after ~100–200 m, so it must get close first by stalking or ambush. [S76]
- **Hunting success:** ~25% overall. A lone lion hunting by day succeeds 17–19% of the time, versus ~30% for group hunts. [S4] (V)
- **Where kills happen:** in cover, erosion gullies and near water, not where prey is densest. [S5] (V)
- **Activity:** rests 18–20 hours a day and hunts mostly at night and dawn. [S76]
- **Groups:** prides of ~3–30, averaging ~15. Males form coalitions of 1–4+. [S76]
- **Roar:** heard at ~8 km. Given mostly at night and dawn, in bouts of several roars, many times a night.
- **Other sounds:** grunts for contact, growls and snarls for threat.
- **Fear of humans:** the 2023 Kruger study used playbacks at waterholes. Savanna animals fled human voices about twice as often as lion sounds. [S6] (V)
- **Near people:** lions usually avoid people on foot and retreat.
- **Charges:** a growling mock charge ends at a few metres if the person stands firm. Running away triggers a chase.
- **Cornered or wounded:** an extremely dangerous charge.
- **Lionesses with cubs:** hide them in dens and are highly aggressive near them.
- **Seasonal:** during dry-season concentrations they ambush at waterholes. Rainy-season dispersal lowers success. [S5] (V)

### Leopard
- **Speed:** ~58 km/h in short bursts. Hunts by stalking within ~5–10 m, then a short rush or pounce. [S10]
- **Hunting success:** ~14–38% depending on site; ~28% for a female with cubs and ~14% for a lone male. [S4] (V)
- **Senses:** excellent night vision and hearing. [S10]
- **Activity:** mostly nocturnal and crepuscular. Rests in trees or dense cover by day. [S10]
- **Groups:** solitary and territorial.
- **Kill hoisting:** hoists kills into trees, including ones heavier than itself, to keep them from lions and hyenas. [S11]
- **Near people:** extremely secretive and avoids people, slipping away unseen. Rarely shows itself.
- **Danger to people:** wounded or cornered leopards charge fast and low and are famously dangerous. Man-eating occurs in parts of India.
- **With cubs:** cubs are hidden for ~8 weeks, and mothers are aggressive near dens.
- **Sounds:** a rasping "sawing" call, like wood being sawn, of ~10–15 strokes. Used for territory, mostly at night and dawn. Growls and hisses for threat. [S10]
- **Weather:** hunts in rain; noise cover helps it.

### Tiger
- **Speed:** ~50–65 km/h in short bursts. An ambush hunter that stalks to ~10–20 m, then makes a rush of under ~100 m. [S12]
- **Hunting success:** ~5–10% in classic Indian studies. Amur tigers did better: 38% on red deer and 54% on wild boar. [S4] (V)
- **Activity:** mostly nocturnal and crepuscular. Rests by day, often in water in hot weather. [S12]
- **Groups:** solitary and territorial, marking with scent and scrapes. [S12]
- **Travel:** covers ~10–20+ km per night.
- **Near people:** usually avoids people.
- **Man-eating:** man-eaters (often injured or old tigers, or in places like the Sundarbans) attack from behind. Masks worn on the back of the head deterred attacks in the Sundarbans. [S72]
- **Wounded or cornered:** a ferocious charge.
- **Cubs:** stay with the mother ~2 years, and she is very aggressive near them.
- **Sounds:** roar heard ~3 km. "Aaonh" contact calls; "chuffing" (prusten) as a friendly greeting; snarls, growls and coughs in attack.
- **Weather:** loves water and swims well. In the heat it rests in streams.

### Spotted hyena
- **Speed:** travels at ~10 km/h and exceeds 50 km/h in pursuit. [S7] (V)
- **Chase hunting:** runs prey down over long chases, often 1–3+ km. Hyenas hunt more than they scavenge in many areas. [S7]
- **Activity:** mostly nocturnal, peaking near midnight and before sunrise. Spends 50–80% of its time resting. [S7] (V)
- **Range:** travels ~27 km per night, up to ~80 km. [S7] (V)
- **Clans:** 8–80; about 8 in the Kalahari and 55–75 in the Serengeti. Clans are female-dominated. [S7] (V)
- **Whoop:** heard by humans at 5+ km. During inter-clan exchanges a clan whoops about every 2 minutes, for up to nearly an hour. [S7] (V)
- **Other sounds:** "giggles" for excitement or submission at kills, plus growls, grunts and lowing. [S7]
- **Near people:** cautious with people on foot but bold at night around camps and villages. It will scavenge, bite sleeping people in rare cases, and steal food. [S6]
- **In groups:** mobs lions at kills when hyenas outnumber them. [S7]
- **Cubs:** raised in communal dens; mothers defend them.
- **Weather:** largely unaffected.

### Honey badger
- **Movement:** trots tirelessly, ~30 km/h at most. Digs extremely fast. *(speed is my estimate, not checked against a source)* [S13]
- **Senses:** keen smell is used to find buried prey and bee nests. [S13]
- **Activity:** nocturnal where people are around, otherwise active by day. Travels ~20–40 km per night. [S13]
- **Groups:** solitary; mothers with one cub. [S13]
- **Near people:** usually ignores or avoids people.
- **When threatened:** famously aggressive. It turns and charges with a rattling growl, bites, and releases a foul secretion from its anal gland. [S13] [S75]
- **Toughness:** its thick, loose skin lets it twist and bite back even when grabbed. [S75]
- **Takes on predators:** takes on lions, leopards and hyenas; reportedly only big cats kill adults. [S75]
- **Raiding:** raids beehives and camp food, and is persistent and clever around camps. [S75]
- **Sounds:** grunts and a loud "rattle" growl or roar when threatened, plus hisses. *(the bark detail is not from a source)*
- **Weather:** shelters in burrows in cold or heat.

### Warthog
- **Speed:** ~50 km/h, running with its tail held straight up. [S14]
- **Burrows:** uses aardvark burrows and enters them backwards, tusks facing out. [S14]
- **Activity:** strictly diurnal; sleeps in burrows at night. [S14]
- **Groups:** "sounders" of females and young (~4–15). Males are solitary or form bachelor groups. [S14]
- **Near people:** flees early, at ~50–100 m.
- **Cornered:** fights with its tusks, and boars slash. A charge from a burrow is a risk. [S14]
- **With young:** mothers defend piglets and will charge jackals and even cheetahs.
- **Rut:** in the dry season boars fight head-to-head; the facial "warts" protect them. [S14]
- **Predators:** lions and leopards. Leopards and lions also dig them out of burrows.
- **Sounds:** grunts, squeals and snorts; a loud squeal when caught.
- **Weather:** rests in burrows in the midday heat and on cold nights; mud-wallows.

### White rhino / black rhino
- **Speed:** ~40–50 km/h charges. [S15]
- **Senses:**
  - Poor eyesight; a still person beyond ~30 m is barely seen.
  - Superb smell and hearing, so wind direction matters.
  - Oxpeckers on their backs act as alarm-givers. [S15] [S16]
- **Black rhino:** a solitary browser. Nervous and quick to charge at sounds or smells, often running blindly toward the source and then veering. [S15] [S16]
- **White rhino:** a placid grazer in groups ("crashes") of up to ~10–15. It usually runs away, tail curled, rather than charging. [S16]
- **Activity:** feeds at dawn, dusk and night. Wallows and rests at midday. [S15]
- **With calves:** black rhino calves run behind the mother, white rhino calves in front. Mothers are very protective.
- **Bulls:** fight with horns and are territorial, spray-urinating and scraping dung middens. [S16]
- **Sounds:**
  - Snorts and puffs for alarm.
  - Mewing calls and squeals from calves.
  - Growls and roars from bulls.
  - A loud screaming snort when charging.
- **Humans:** fled human voices more than lions in the Kruger study. [S6] (V)
- **Weather:** wallows in heat; mud protects against sun and insects.

### African elephant
- **Speed:** maximum ~25–40 km/h in a fast "run", without true galloping. Walks ~4–6 km/h for long distances.
- **Senses:**
  - Smell is the best sense; they detect water and people at several km.
  - Hearing is excellent, and they also sense seismic vibrations through the feet.
  - Eyesight is moderate. [S8] [S9]
- **Rumble range:** typically heard by other elephants at 2–4 km by day and 7–10 km on still, cool evenings. Individuals recognise each other's rumbles at ~1–1.5 km. [S8] (V)
- **Call types:** rumbles, roars, cries, barks, snorts and trumpets. Trumpets signal excitement, threat or distress. [S8] (V)
- **Fear response:** elephants were the one species that fled lion sounds more than human voices. [S6] (V)
- **Groups:** matriarchal families of ~6–20, which merge into bond groups and clans of 100+. Bulls are solitary or in bachelor groups.
- **Near people:** alert posture with ears spread, head up and trunk raised to smell.
- **Mock charge:** ears out, head shaking and dust kicked, stopping short.
- **Real charge:** ears pinned back, trunk curled in, silent, head down.
- **Herd defence:** families with calves bunch around them and charge predators and people.
- **Musth (bulls):**
  - Temporal glands stream.
  - Bulls dribble urine and give a low-frequency musth rumble.
  - Testosterone is high, so they are aggressive and dangerous for weeks to months.
  - Musth bulls detect and turn toward musth rumbles at 100 m+. [S8] (V)
- **Fire:** wary of fire, but habituated crop-raiders ignore fires and noise.
- **Weather:** in drought they dig for water. In heat they bathe, mud-wallow and flap their ears.

### Woolly mammoth (from elephant analogy and paleontology)
- **Social structure:** ancient DNA sexing shows a strong male bias among fossils. This suggests elephant-like matriarchal herds, with risk-taking young males alone or in bachelor groups dying more often in traps such as bogs and crevasses. [S63]
- **Ice Age hunting:** evidence suggests Ice Age humans may have preferentially hunted females and young. [S74]
- **Movement and senses:** elephant analogues apply. Walks ~4–6 km/h; mock charges and real charges as in elephants; smell is the best sense; infrasonic rumbles are likely.
- **Tusk evidence:** tusks show seasonal growth rings, which record migrations and musth-like annual stress in males. [S63]
- **Weather:** cold-adapted, with thick fur, small ears and a fat hump. In winter it likely sought windswept steppe to graze.
- **Fire:** no direct evidence. Model it on elephants: wary.

## DOMESTIC / FERAL

### Domestic cat (feral / stray)
- **Speed:** sprints ~45 km/h over short distances. Walks ~1 m/s.
- **Senses:** acute hearing (up to ultrasonic) and excellent low-light vision. Whiskers are used for touch at close range.
- **Hunting:** stalk and pounce. Success is ~32% overall for feral cats in northern Australia, rising to ~70% in open habitat. [S4] (V) [S64]
- **Activity:** crepuscular and nocturnal; sleeps 12–16+ hours a day. [S64]
- **Groups:** solitary hunters. Colonies form around food (related females). [S64]
- **Near people:** feral cats flee to cover at ~10–30 m.
- **Cornered:** arched back, puffed fur, hiss, spit, growl, then strikes with claws and bites.
- **With kittens:** the mother hides them and is defensive.
- **Mating:** caterwauling, yowling and fights in the breeding season; noisy at night.
- **Sounds:** meowing is mostly directed at humans. Hiss and growl for threat. Purring.
- **Weather:** shelters from rain and seeks warmth. Hunts in wind (noise cover).

### Dog (feral / stray, free-roaming)
- **Speed:** ~30–50 km/h depending on build. Trots for hours.
- **Senses:** smell is overwhelmingly dominant, then hearing; vision is good for movement.
- **Groups:** free-roaming village dogs are mostly solitary or pairs with loose small groups. True feral packs are ~2–10. [S66] [S65]
- **Range:** home ranges vary from a few hundred metres for village dogs to many km² for feral packs. [S66] [S65]
- **Attacks on stock:** feral and wild-dog packs attack sheep and other livestock, often maiming many animals in chase. They test prey by running it. [S65]
- **Activity:** crepuscular and nocturnal where persecuted, more diurnal near people. [S66]
- **Near people:** strays are often wary and keep ~10–50 m away.
- **Packs and territory:** packs and territorial dogs bark, advance, and may bite from behind if a person runs.
- **Scavenging:** dogs drawn to food scavenge near settlements and camps.
- **Females:** guard their dens.
- **Sounds:** barking for alarm or territory, which is frequent and contagious. Howling for contact, especially at night. Growls, whines and yelps.
- **Weather:** shelters in rain and heat; more active in cool weather.

- **Human voices trigger flight:** across 95% of the savanna species tested, animals fled human voices about twice as readily as lion sounds. Elephants were the exception. [S6] (V)
