# Cirques and horns: headwall retreat rates and how many glacial cycles make a horn

**Last checked:** 2026-11-08 (web research by Jamie, for Sonny). **System owner:** Sonny (the landform and glacial stages, `tools/watershed/glacial.py`, the pyramid-peak rule and the B2 headwall retreat rate). **Related:** `docs/water/watershed_rebuild_design.md`, `snow-lines-mountain-climate.md` (#594), `snow-on-slopes.md` (#632).

"Our sums" means arithmetic done here from a sourced number. "Judged" means no source was found today.

## Lessons (read these first)

1. **Long-term cirque headwall retreat is about 0.1 to 1.3 mm a year. Most measured sites sit near or below 1 mm a year.**
   - **Barr et al. (2019, Earth Surface Processes and Landforms), Table 1**, compiled cirque erosion rates in mm a year (headward means headwall retreat, vertical means floor lowering):

     | Site | Headward | Vertical |
     |---|---|---|
     | Kråkenes, Norway | 0.1 | 0.5 to 0.6 |
     | Ben Ohau Range, New Zealand | 0.44 | 0.29 |
     | Canadian Rockies, BC | 1.2 | 0.5 to 0.9 |
     | Marie Byrd Land, Antarctica (volcanic breccia) | 5.8 | 0.4 |

     - Other floor-lowering rates: Rocky Mountains 0.15 to 1.8, Arapaho Glacier 0.1 to 0.17, Pangnirtung (Baffin) 0.008 to 0.08, Øvre Beiarbreen 0.18, and Ivory Lake and Nisqually 5 to 6 under fast temperate ice.
     - **Britain and Ireland:** about 0.14 mm a year if cirques grew evenly over the whole Quaternary.
     - **Pyrenees:** 0.03 to 0.35 mm a year under cirque glaciers, but only 0 to 0.03 under an icefield. Cirques grow when they hold small glaciers, not when buried under an ice sheet.
   - **Ben Ohau** (Brook, Kirkbride and Brock 2006): "For cirques with up to c. 750 ka occupancy, rates of downcutting average 0·29 mm a⁻¹ and headwall retreat 0·44 mm a⁻¹."
   - **Helmet Mountain, Canadian Rockies** (Sanders 2011, UC Berkeley thesis): "subaerial headward retreat of the headwall occurred at ~1.3 mm/yr (0.2 - 5 mm/yr)". The cirque "elongated and deepened at roughly equivalent rates of order 1 mm/yr" over the past few centuries.
   - **Kitzsteinhorn, Austria** (Hartmeyer et al. 2020, lidar 2011-2017, Bündner schist): a deglaciating cirque wall retreats at 1.86 mm a year on average, 7.6 mm a year within 10 m of the ice and 0.9 beyond it. The authors say long-term cirque rates elsewhere "all report (long-term) rockwall retreat rates around or below 1 mm a−1". Their high rates are a short burst after the ice thinned.
2. **Granite: Lochnagar corrie retreated at about 1 mm a year while glaciers were active. That needed about 400 kyr of active glaciation, spread over many glacial cycles.**
   - **Hall et al. (2024, Journal of Quaternary Science)**, Cairngorms granite:
     - Younger Dryas moraine volumes give "a rate of cliff retreat of ~1.0 m ka−1". At that rate "a total of ~400 ka of active glaciation was necessary for erosion of Lochnagar Corrie".
     - The Younger Dryas glacier "is only one phase of a possible eight during the last glacial cycle", and "erosion of Lochnagar Corrie was incremental through multiple Pleistocene glaciations".
     - The granite plateau around it lost only about 1.6 mm per thousand years (0.0016 mm a year), mostly to frost, with glacial erosion "restricted to block removal from upstanding tors". In granite, the cirques bite into the edge while the summits barely change.
3. **A cirque gets most of its size in its first glaciation. Later cycles grow it about ten times more slowly.**
   - **Barr et al. (2019):** published times to form a cirque range from about 125 kyr to over a million years.
   - **Tuya Butte, British Columbia:** formed about 140 ka. It has two cirques 185 m and 210 m deep, so they must have formed within one glacial cycle. Growth then "reportedly slows by an order of magnitude".
   - **Occupancy and size:** cirque depth tracks time under small glaciers weakly (r² = 0.49) and shows no link at all with time under ice sheets (r² = 0.00). Scottish cirques held ice for more than 100 kyr of the last cycle, but they are not deeper than Irish or Welsh ones held for under 40 kyr.
4. **Cirques eat sideways into ranges faster than valleys cut down.**
   - **Oskin and Burbank (2005, Geology), Kyrgyz Range, Tien Shan:** cirques drove the divide back "at rates of as much as two to three times the rate of valley incision": 0.9 to 4.4 km of headward retreat against 0.4 to 1.3 km of valley incision.
   - The crest stays pinned near the glacier level (about 4,000 m there). Cirque retreat can "bevel across an elevated alpine plateau".
5. **A horn is where three or more cirque headwalls meet** (Wikipedia, "Pyramidal peak"). "When three or more of these cirques converge on a central point, they create a pyramid-shaped peak with steep walls". Most horns have three or four faces.
   - **The Matterhorn:** carved by "the Quaternary period of glacial erosion" (International Journal of Geoheritage and Parks 2023). No source today gives a count of cycles for it.
   - **Arran, granite about 60 Ma:** "Successive glaciations then shaped the landscape". Glen Rosa is "a textbook U-shaped glaciated valley with corries, aretes, hanging valleys and horn peaks" (National Trust for Scotland, Goatfell geology).
   - **Skye's Cuillin, gabbro:** corries formed by radial ice flow (Coire na Banachdich, Coire Lagan, Coir'-uisg). In the main ice sheet phase, the ice "completely enveloped even the highest summits". A late-glacial readvance (10,800 to 10,300 years BP) put a small ice cap back on the Cuillin (BGS Earthwise, "Quaternary and Recent geology of the Isle of Skye").
6. **How many cycles there were:**
   - **Mid-Pleistocene Transition** (Wikipedia): glacial cycles were about 41 kyr long, low and thin, before about 1.25 to 0.7 Ma. Since then they have been about 100 kyr long, asymmetric and large.
   - **Our sums:** that is about 7 to 12 cycles of 100 kyr.
   - **Norway** (Geological Society of Norway, "The making of a land", ch. 15): more than 40 glaciations in all, with "almost 50" smaller ones between 2.6 Ma and 0.9 Ma. Local mountain glaciers were present for about 1.2 Myr in total of the last 2.6 Myr. Sognefjorden was cut to 2,000 m depth over repeated glaciations.

7. **The 41 kyr world was milder and more even than the 100 kyr world. Its cirques spent much of their time with glaciers in them.**
   - **Symmetric and small:**
     - early Pleistocene cycles "appear symmetric with smaller ice volumes and a period of 41 kyr" (PAGES Magazine);
     - before 2.5 Myr, the rates of ice growth and melt were "nearly identical". After that, melt got faster than growth, "eventually creating the pronounced saw-tooth shape of late Pleistocene glacial cycles" (Lisiecki and Raymo 2007);
     - the 41 kyr cycles had "low-amplitude, thin ice sheets" (Wikipedia, Mid-Pleistocene Transition).
   - **In between most of the time:**
     - "For most of Quaternary time, environments were intermediate in character". Under these average conditions, mountain glaciers had a snowline about 500 m below today's (Porter 1989);
     - Porter names Pacific Northwest cirques as landforms of such average conditions rather than full glacials.
   - **Mountain glaciers in Norway** were present for about 1.2 Myr in total over 2.6 Myr (lesson 6). That is 46% of the time, across both worlds.

8. **Poleward- and lee-facing cirques sit lower: about 110 to 220 m between the best and worst aspect.**
   - In the Făgăraş Mountains (Romania), cirques facing NNW are 113 m lower than those facing SSE (Barr and Spagnolo 2015).
   - In SW British Columbia, predicted north to south cirque floor differences are 194 m in the Bendor Range and 220 m in the Cayoosh Range (Evans 2006, cited by Barr and Spagnolo 2015).
   - In Kamchatka:
     - cirques facing SSE are "typically 163 m higher than those facing NNW";
     - modern glacier mid-altitudes are 190 m lower facing 311° (NW) than facing 131° (SE);
     - aspect explains only a small part of the spread, r² = 0.03 (Barr and Spagnolo 2015, Kamchatka).
   - The best aspect is north to north-east in the northern hemisphere, and so **south to south-east on our starter island at 57° S** (`one-sky.md`). Shade adds to snow blown over onto the lee of the westerlies (`snow-on-slopes.md`).

## What this means for the pyramid-peak rule and B2 (our sums and judged)

### Headwall retreat rate (B2)

| Use | Rate |
|---|---|
| While a cirque glacier is active, hard crystalline rock (granite, gabbro) | **0.5 to 1.0 mm a year**. Lochnagar about 1.0, Ben Ohau 0.44, Helmet Mountain about 1.3 in weaker rock. Judged mid value 0.7 |
| While buried under an ice sheet | about 0 to 0.03 (Pyrenees). Cirques don't grow then |
| Averaged over the whole Quaternary, all phases included | about 0.1 to 0.15 (the Britain and Ireland figure) |
| Short bursts right after deglaciation | 2 to 10 mm a year for decades. Not a long-term rate |

If B2 is a rate during active cirque glaciation, 1 mm a year in granite is at the top of the sourced range, and 0.5 to 0.7 is the middle. If B2 is averaged over whole cycles, it should be about 0.1 to 0.15 (or the active rate times the share of each cycle with cirque glaciers, below).

### Active cirque time per 100 kyr cycle

- **Judged: about 30 to 50 kyr per cycle.** Cirque glaciers live in the build-up and decay phases. Under the full ice sheet, cirques barely grow.
- **Our sums:** Lochnagar's 400 kyr of active glaciation divided by 30 to 50 kyr per cycle is about 8 to 13 cycles. That fits the 7 to 12 cycles of 100 kyr since the Mid-Pleistocene Transition.

- **Our sums for the whole history**, at the suggested values:
  - total active ice stays about 1.04 Myr (39 × 16.4 + 10 × 40 kyr);
  - at 0.5 to 1.0 mm a year, that is 0.5 to 1.0 km of headwall retreat, the same as in Sonny's plan;
  - a snowline 150 m higher in the 41 kyr world shrinks the cirques' reach downhill by about 150 m for 39 of the 49 cycles. Cirque floors that sit within 150 m of the 100 kyr snowline then get only the 100 kyr world's 400 kyr.
- **Check after the run:**
  - the SE to NW (best to worst face) headwall retreat ratio should come out at 1.5 to 2 (the "Where" line below);
  - SE cirque floors should sit about 100 to 200 m below NW ones on the starter (57° S).

### How far a headwall must retreat to make a horn

A horn forms when three or more headwalls close in until only a pyramid is left. Our sums from the rates above:

| Headwall retreat needed | Active time at 0.5 mm/a | Active time at 1.0 mm/a | Cycles of 100 kyr (40 kyr active each) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 m (a small horn, cirques already close) | 400 kyr | 200 kyr | 5 to 10 |
| 500 m (an Arran or Cuillin scale ridge and horn) | 1.0 Myr | 500 kyr | 12 to 25 |
| 1 km (a Matterhorn scale horn, faces about 1 km) | 2.0 Myr | 1.0 Myr | 25 to 50 |

- **Plus the first cycle:** the first cycle cuts the cirque basin itself, about 150 to 200 m deep (lesson 3). That is a fast start, not counted in the retreat above.
- **What the table says:**
  - the post-1 Ma 100 kyr cycles alone (7 to 12) make small horns and arêtes in granite;
  - a Matterhorn-scale horn needs about 1 to 2 Myr of active cirque time, so it needs the earlier 41 kyr cycles too (about 1.2 Myr of mountain glaciers in Norway since 2.6 Ma, lesson 6);
  - Alpine relief and fast uplift help (lesson 4).

### Suggested numbers for the pyramid-peak rule

- **Cycles** (judged):
  - **10 cycles of 100 kyr** is the honest count for the post-1 Ma record, and it gives Arran and Cuillin style arêtes and small horns.
  - **20 cycles**, or more for the colossal starter peaks, if the rule should also cover the earlier, smaller 41 kyr glaciations. That is the reading that matches Dave's "colossal epic peaks" (D-10).
- **Per cycle:**
  - cirque deepening fast in cycle 1 (about 150 to 200 m), then about 10 times slower;
  - headwall retreat of 20 to 40 m per cycle (0.5 to 1 mm a year × 40 kyr active);
  - summits and plateaux in granite barely lowered (lesson 2: about 0.16 m per 100 kyr).
- **When a summit becomes a horn:** three or more cirque headwalls within about 200 to 400 m of the summit, measured in plan (judged). Two facing headwalls make an arête.
- **Where:** horns form where cirques were busiest. On a mid-latitude island that means the poleward- to east-facing sides (south to south-east on the starter at 57° S) (shade and lee snow, `snow-on-slopes.md`), so cirques on those sides should retreat about 1.5 to 2 times faster (judged).

### Never-see checks

- a horn on a peak with cirques on only one side;
- a granite plateau lowered as fast as the cirque walls cutting into it;
- cirque floors deepening at the same rate every cycle;
- deep cirques on sunny, windward slopes when the shaded lee slopes have none.
