## Lessons (read these first)

1. **Ice strength goes with the square of its thickness. A person needs about 10 cm of clear ice, and most of our animals need less.**
   - Gold's formula: the load ice can carry is P = A × h². The Minnesota DNR's table uses a bearing strength of 50 psi and gives 800 lb on 4 inches (10 cm) of solid, clear ice, rising to 20,000 lb on 20 inches. Its figures "assume solid, clear ice", and "ice is NEVER 100% safe" (Minnesota DNR, Ice thickness guidelines by weight).
   - **Our sums:** in metric, the DNR's table is P (kg) = 3.5 × h (cm)².
     - The common rule of 10 cm for one person walking is then a safety factor of about 4 on an 80 kg person.
     - Using a quarter of that, P_safe = 0.9 × h², gives the 10 cm rule back.
   - **Ice each animal needs** (our sums, by body mass):

     | Animal | Mass | May hold (3.5 h²) | Holds for sure (0.9 h²) |
     |---|---|---|---|
     | Raven | 1.2 kg | 0.6 cm | 1.2 cm |
     | Hare | 3.5 kg | 1.0 cm | 2 cm |
     | Fox | 6 kg | 1.3 cm | 2.6 cm |
     | Roe deer | 25 kg | 2.7 cm | 5.3 cm |
     | Wolf | 40 kg | 3.4 cm | 6.7 cm |
     | Sheep | 60 kg | 4.1 cm | 8.2 cm |
     | Person | 80 kg | 4.8 cm | 9.4 cm (the 10 cm rule) |
     | Red deer hind, boar | 90 kg | 5.1 cm | 10 cm |
     | Red deer stag | 150 kg | 6.5 cm | 13 cm |

   - White (snow) ice is weaker than clear ice (the DNR assumes clear). The common rule of thumb is to count white ice as half (judged, not fetched).

2. **Deer on smooth ice fall and cannot get up. Chased deer run onto thin ice.**
   - Rescues of deer stuck on frozen lakes are reported every winter. In a BC case, a buck "was likely chased by coyotes onto the lake, which in some parts, was only two-inches thick". It was found "splayed out on all fours", and would have "succumbed to hypothermia" or been taken by predators that night (*Cottage Life*, BC conservation officers save deer).
     - In that case the fog hid the officers, so the deer did not panic and break through.
   - **Our reading** (judged): a hoof has a small hard edge and no grip on bare, smooth ice. The legs slide out sideways, and once down on its belly a deer cannot get its feet under it. On snow-covered or rough ice it walks well enough.
   - Deer avoid bare ice when they can, but panic or a chase overrides that.

3. **Wolves use frozen lakes as roads, and sometimes as a hunting ground.**
   - Isle Royale's wolves arrived over the ice: "a few wolves, possibly only a single pair, crossed an ice bridge from Ontario ... during a harsh winter" in 1949. A later wolf "crossed over the ice from mainland Ontario, 15 miles away", and in 2018 a released female "left the island via an ice bridge" (Wikipedia, Wolves and moose on Isle Royale).
   - **Judged:**
     - wolves trot along frozen lakes and rivers because it is flat, open and snow is often wind-packed there;
     - they drive deer onto bare ice, where the deer slip.
     - Wolves do fall through: an Isle Royale wolf survived a plunge into Lake Superior (Star Tribune, headline only, not read).

4. **Light animals float on snow, and heavy ones sink.**
   - Foot load (body weight per area of foot, Murray and Boutin 1991, Yukon):
     - adult lynx 26 to 32 g/cm², snowshoe hare 17 to 18, coyote 106 to 137;
     - coyotes sank 9.5 ± 4.4 cm a step and lynx 7.7 ± 3.2 cm, in snow 40 to 55 cm deep. Sinking "rarely" passed 20 cm, well below chest height;
     - coyotes spent about half their travel (52%) on animal trails and lynx 37%. The heavier-footed animal uses packed trails more.
   - Deer have far higher foot loads, about 400 to 500 g/cm² (from memory of Telfer and Kelsall 1984; not fetched). So deer sink to the belly in deep soft snow, while foxes and hares stay near the top. Telfer and Kelsall found the species of snowier regions have lower foot loads and longer legs for their size.
   - **Judged for deer:** snow over about 50 cm hampers deer, and they gather in sheltered conifers on packed trails (a "yard"). Wolves follow the packed trails too.

5. **Snow lying unmelted on an animal's back means its coat is holding the heat in.**
   - Judged, from common naturalists' observation, not fetched: a deer, boar or sheep in a snowfall can carry a white layer on its back that does not melt, because the thick winter coat lets little heat out.
   - Wet, thin or sick animals melt it and look dark and soaked instead.

6. **Water birds stand on ice at the edge of open water.**
   - Judged, not fetched:
     - ducks, geese, swans and gulls rest on the ice next to a hole or open lead, and slip into the water when disturbed;
     - herons stand at the ice edge to fish;
     - on a pond frozen right over, water birds leave for open water or the sea;
     - dippers and kingfishers keep to the running water that stays open below falls and at springs.

## Judged numbers for Steve and MikeW

### Who goes on the ice

| Animal | Crosses frozen water? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fox, hare, birds | yes, freely, from 2 to 3 cm | light: prints show on snowy ice |
| Wolf | yes, as a road, from about 7 cm | trots along it; chases deer onto it |
| Roe deer | only when chased, or on snowy ice from about 5 cm | slips on bare ice: falls, struggles, and stays down |
| Red deer, boar, sheep, cattle | rarely, only when chased, from about 10 cm | heavy: breaks through under 5 cm |
| Bear | avoids it (judged) | the heaviest |
| Ducks, gulls, herons | stand at the ice edge by open water | leave a pond frozen right over |

- **A break-through rule** (judged, from lesson 1):
  - ice holds for sure if mass ≤ 0.9 × h²;
  - it breaks if mass > 3.5 × h²;
  - in between, it holds with a chance falling from 1 to 0 across the band.
  - Halve h for white (snow) ice and near springs and inflows, where the ice is thinner (judged).
- **On the starter island:** at most 2.8 cm at the spawn pond and 5.6 cm by spring #8002 (the coordinator's #731 note).
  - These figures hold because ice reads the starter's `snow_bias_c` of −6 °C (`OVERNIGHT_DECISIONS_8NOV.md` row 21, #717). So for ice the island behaves like about 8.4 °C at sea level, close to the old 9 °C, even though the plain air follows the grid's 14.4 °C.
  - The spring's own pool stays open: its water is about 10.9 °C (`springs-and-brooks-in-winter.md`, #739).
  - 2.8 cm carries birds, hares and foxes for sure, and a roe deer at best. Nothing heavier.
  - 5.6 cm carries a fox for sure and may carry a wolf or a roe deer. It breaks under red deer, boar and people.
  - So on the starter's low ground the ice is for small animals only. That fits "dark skim only" for people. Higher up, colder ice grows thicker, and high tarns can bear a person in midwinter (judged).

### Slipping

- **Bare, smooth ice:** hoofed animals fall. Judged: one fall in about 3 strides when running, and a deer down on bare ice stays down until helped or until it slides to the edge.
- **Snow on the ice, or rough ice:** no extra falls.
- **Paws** (wolf, fox): no falls on any ice (judged).

### Snow on their backs

- In a snowfall, a healthy animal with a winter coat gathers a white layer on its back and head over about 15 to 30 game minutes. It stays until the animal shakes, which it does when it moves on (judged).
- A wet or hurt animal does not gather it.

### Sinking in snow

| Animal | Foot load | Sinks in soft snow |
|---|---|---|
| Hare | 17 to 18 g/cm² | a few cm |
| Lynx (as fox) | 26 to 32 g/cm² | about 8 cm |
| Coyote (as wolf, a little lower) | 106 to 137 g/cm² | about 10 cm |
| Deer | about 400 to 500 g/cm² (memory) | to the belly in deep soft snow; hampered over about 50 cm |

- Sourced rows: Murray and Boutin 1991, measured in snow 40 to 55 cm deep.
- **Packed trails:** heavier-footed animals use them more. In the study, coyotes used them 52% of the time and lynx 37%. Wolves and deer should keep to their own trails in deep snow.
- **Print life in snow:** see [animal-tracks-sign.md](animal-tracks-sign.md): until melt or new snow, filled by drifting.

## Never-see checks

- a deer trotting calmly across bare ice;
- a red deer or a person walking on 3 cm of pond ice;
- foxes or birds afraid of firm ice;
- a wolf sinking deeper in snow than a deer;
- snow lying on the back of a soaked or hurt animal;
- ducks sitting in the middle of a pond frozen right over, far from any open water.
