## 1. Why the current approach fails

I traced the bake from the first stage: `world_elevation.gd`, then `river_network.gd`, `tile_height.gd`, `tools/erosion_precarve`, `tile_baker.gd` and `tile_water.gd` / `tile_drainage.gd`, then the passes after the water. The order today is:

1. **World elevation, 160 m cells** (`world_elevation.gd`): island shapes, mountains, shelves, craters, bays.
2. **World rivers, 160 m** (`river_network.gd`): a priority flood that makes river chains, then smooth curves with a random sideways "meander" push. They are used as **valley guides**, so the tile height cuts valleys around them.
3. **Tile height, 8 m** (`tile_height.gd`): cubic upsampling, valleys cut around the guides, detail noise (several octaves), summit sharpening.
4. **Erosion pre-carve**: an offline landscape model (`tools/erosion_precarve`, stream power, Braun and Willett 2013) run on the pre-erosion heights at 16 to 32 m. It is stored as a **delta** (after minus before), then soft-capped (fill at most 12 m), smoothed where steep, faded at the frame edge, and added to the 8 m ground.
5. **Strata ledges, then scree aprons**, added after the erosion.
6. **Rolling swells and hills** (`tile_rolling.gd`, up to about 80 m summed). Before P1 they were added after the water.
7. **Water** (`tile_drainage.gd`): routing over the result, then lakes. Channels are carved by rule (`drainage_channels.gd`: fixed extra depth, bank dykes, bank cones, head ponds, spring caves by rule).
8. **Eight repair passes after the water**: pond levels clamped to their spill, river mend (digging through ridges), join corners, river mouths (twice), valley drain (raising land beside rivers), drain hollows (filling dry pits), spring and cave sites by rule.

Why this cannot give the results in section 14:

- **Three different river systems.** The 160 m world rivers shape the valleys, the erosion model makes its own drainage, and the tile drainage routes again over ground the other two never saw. They disagree. The pre-carve README warns of this itself: "32 m routing can disagree with 8 m routing, so rivers may leave carved valleys".
- **The ground is changed after it was drained.** The erosion result is applied as a capped, smoothed delta onto a different surface (the fine detail noise it never saw). Then ledges, scree and hills are added on top. Each of those adds closed hollows and dams. Dino's census on tile 3,4 (7 Nov) measured this: 43,826 hollow cells before the rolling step, and 6 times the hollow volume after it, up to 23.6 m deep.
- **Water placed by rule, not by supply.** Channel depth is a fixed number (5 m extra, at most 8 m), whatever the flow. Every head was given a pond or a cave. Lakes are kept or dried by a catchment threshold, not a water balance. The watershed document names all three as things we should never see: uniform trenches, deep gullies under tiny flows, and every stream beginning at a head pond or a cave mouth.
- **Repairs instead of causes.** Each repair pass fixes one symptom and makes another. The TERRAIN_VERSION history (44 on 30 Oct to 61 on 7 Nov) is that loop.
- **Brooks are carved into 8 m cells.** A 1 to 2 m brook cut into 8 m cells becomes an 8 m trench. The document says "Avoid carving uniformly deep trenches under every stream".
- **Nothing models groundwater, seasons or wetland saturation.** So springs, seeps, wetlands and flow regimes cannot be placed for a natural reason. Sections 2, 3, 7 and 8 of the document need these.

The cure is not another pass. It is a new order: **landform, rock and climate first; then one landscape-evolution and hydrology model that makes the ground and the water together; then the tiles read its result.**

## 2. Design principles (from the watershed document)

1. **One drainage truth.** One flow-routing graph, on the final ground, gives every basin, divide, channel, lake and outlet. No second routing anywhere.
2. **Ground and water are made together.** Valleys come from the water that carved them: the erosion model's own channels. Nothing raises or cuts the ground after the hydrology, except sub-cell channel detail drawn from the same record (section 4.6).
3. **Size follows supply.** Discharge = rain-weighted catchment, minus losses. Width, depth and valley shape come from discharge and slope by measured relations, not from fixed numbers.
4. **Water appears for a reason.** Channel heads come from a slope-area threshold. Springs and seeps come from a groundwater model. Lakes come from basins and a water balance. Wetlands come from saturation. Cave springs come only from soluble or fractured rock with enough catchment.
5. **Variety from conditions.** Rock, rain and slope differ between catchments, so their water differs. No random templates.
6. **Seams by construction.** The whole baked island is solved on one grid, and tiles sample it by world position.
7. **One water budget for ground and water.** The same runoff (rain, minus evaporation, split by the rock into quick runoff and groundwater recharge) drives the erosion that shapes the valleys and the rivers that later run in them. If the erosion used all the rain while the rivers used only what runs off, chalk, limestone and dry slopes would get valleys cut by water that is not there: deep valleys with no stream, which the document's last never-see item forbids (water and ground that disagree).
8. **Every feature can explain itself.** Each lake, spring, seep, head, wetland, reach and outlet carries in the record the answers to the document's seven plausibility questions (section 13): what area and pathways feed it, why it is here and not nearby, what controls its level or outlet, what rock and soil made it, how it behaves in storm, normal weather and drought, what evidence it leaves, and how it reaches the coast or an internal sink. A feature that cannot answer one is a defect.

## 3. The new pipeline

All heavy stages run offline on Dave's PC (Dino), never in the cloud. They are deterministic for a given seed and parameters. One tool, `tools/watershed/` (Python with numba, building on `tools/erosion_precarve`), writes one island record. The Godot tile bake becomes a reader of that record.

### Stage A: landform, rock, climate (inputs, kept from today)
- **A1 macro landform**: `world_elevation.gd` exported as raw heights (the export already exists) with **no** fine detail noise, **no** valley guides and **no** rolling hills. It gives the coastline, the massifs and the base level.
- **A1 peaks that can stand (8 Nov, design for the summit rule).** Island height is set per island type and seed at the landform stage (`summit_m` in `sketch_rules.json`). Erosion carves valleys into it and must never take a summit below its landform height (the coordinator's rule D-10; the starter island's 1,459 m is the floor). Run d9f89615 broke the rule in its first 2,500-year step: the main peak lost 145 m. Neither rivers nor creep can do that in one step. It was the slope limit cutting back a summit steeper than its rock can stand (slate, 44 degrees). No physical erosion can keep such a needle, so the landform must build peaks that can stand.
  - *Why the needle.* Each summit is two shapes stacked:
    - the sketch spire (`world_elevation.gd`: 22% of the summit height over the last 10% of the distance to the sea, power 2.4, on the 160 m sketch grid);
    - the tile tent (`tile_height.gd _add_summits`: 16% of the height, reaching 3.2 times that far, power 1.7).

    Their steepest slopes are both at the tip and add up. The tent alone is power / radius_factor = 0.53 (28 degrees) at its tip; the spire on a 4 km island is over 1 (45 degrees). Together they stand near 60 degrees over a few hundred metres.
  - *Rule 1, standing cones.* At the sketch stage, after all shaping, two passes raise ground (never lower it), so every summit keeps its height and its base widens:
    - *flanks:* every land cell is raised to the highest cone of slope `standing.flank_slope` hanging from any cell above `standing.from_m`, h ← max(h, max over y of (h(y) − slope × distance)), propagated over land only by two chamfer passes. This is the least change that leaves no high ground steeper than the slope. flank_slope = tan(35 degrees, the softest rock's threshold) − 0.05 margin = 0.65;
    - *summit tops:* once the summits are found, the ground within the tile tent's reach (0.64 times the summit height) is raised to a cone of `standing.tip_slope` from the summit, and the summits are found again (they do not move). tip_slope = 0.70 − 0.33 (the tent's tip slope, rule 2) − 0.05 = 0.32, so the tent added on top still stands on any rock.

    Sources below `from_m` are left out, so sea cliffs and low hills are untouched. The sea is never filled, so the coastline does not move. Carved craters keep their walls.
  - *Rule 2, a gentler tent.* power 1.3 and radius_factor 4.0, so its tip slope is 0.33 (18 degrees) and it is still a point.
  - *Rule 3, hard rock under the tall peaks.* Every summit above `summit_pluton_min_m` (900 m) stands on an intrusion whose roof reaches the surface under it: granite, or the vent's basalt on volcanic islands. This is how the high ground of Arran, the Mournes and the Skye Cuillin came about: the hard core stood while the rock around it wore down. The generator places the height first, so the consistent world puts the hard rock where the height is. A granite core holds walls up to 57 degrees, so the valleys can deepen toward the peak without the slope limit reaching the summit. It also brings what granite brings: tin and copper near the contact, tight rock with few springs, and no caves on the core.
  - *Rule 4, summits that are not round (8 Nov, after run c793c47c).* With rules 1 to 3 and the cover fix, the peaks held (the main granite peak lost 27 m in 0.5 Myr), but each tall summit wore into a round dome inside a circular cliff ring: the granite was one disc, the soft rock round it wore away, and the summit top was a cone ending in a ring. Real peaks are cut into faces and ridges, and real batholiths are clusters of plutons with lobed outlines (the Mourne granites G1 to G5, the Cuillin centres). So:
    - each summit gets 3 to 5 *spurs* (`standing.tip_spurs`, directions from the summit's position and the world seed, `WorldElevation.spur_dirs`), shared by the sketch, the tile tent and the geology;
    - the sketch summit top is gentler along a spur (slope × (1 − 0.45) on its crest, a Gaussian of 0.35 rad either side) and steepens from tip_slope to flank_slope by a smoothstep between 0.7 and 1.3 times the reach, so no ring marks where it ends (the drop is the slope's integral, so the top is continuous);
    - the tile tent reaches 60% farther along a spur (`summit.spur_reach`), so its tip slope stays 0.33 on the faces and is gentler on the spurs;
    - the granite is a core (0.6 to 0.75 of the radius) under the summit plus up to 4 lobes, each an ellipse stretched along a spur (radius 0.4 to 0.6 of the pluton radius, aspect 0.45 to 0.75, centred 0.4 to 0.65 out), so the hard rock runs under the ridges and its edge, where the cliffs come, is lobed.
    Every slope stays at or under the rule 1 limits (spurs are only gentler), so the summit rule is unchanged. TERRAIN_VERSION 64.
  - *Rule 5, no generator marks (8 Nov, after the 6 km zoom pictures of run 8a38328c).* The close-ups showed the landform's own construction, and the model's rivers followed it as ruler lines: a star of thin straight rays at every summit, 8-sided flank cones with straight creases along the compass and diagonal lines, and stripes and blocks one or two 160 m sketch cells wide. A real massif has none of these, and the model then reads them as the ground's shape. So:
    - the flank cones of rule 1 use the true distance: each cell carries the cell its cone hangs from and passes it to its neighbours (vector propagation, as in Danielsson's distance transform), not a chamfer of straight and diagonal steps;
    - spurs are not rays: each crest bends by its own amount (up to 0.7 rad over one tip reach, `tip_spurs.bend_rad`, `WorldElevation.spur_crest`), and a spur's width is measured across it in metres (`width_share` of the tip reach at the summit, growing by `widen` metres per metre out), not as an angle, so near the point every direction lies on a spur, the top is round, and the spurs part from it as they fall. An angular width made each spur a wedge pointed at the summit, and the 4edbc722 zooms still showed a star of thin rays even with the spurs faded in near the point; the sketch's own summit top has no spurs (`depth` 0); the granite lobes follow the curved crests;
    - the island's height bands, foothills and coast profile follow the straight-line distance from the sea (an exact Euclidean distance transform, Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher 2012), not a two-pass chamfer: the chamfer's eight-sided distance drew the island's spine as ruler-straight crests along the compass (a straight crest due west from the starter summit in the 4edbc722 zooms);
    - the summit tent falls as (1 − d/reach)² (`summit.power` 2): with 1.3 its slope dropped to nothing in the last few metres and drew a ring round every summit;
    - the landform for the model (`landform_only`) is drawn from the 160 m sketch with a smoothing cubic B-spline, not the Catmull-Rom cubic that passes through every cell and so draws the cell grid. The game's tiles keep Catmull-Rom until the rebuild replaces them.

On the starter island the gentle summit cone reaches about 800 m from the top (the tent's reach), against about 400 m for the spire today, and the flanks below stand at 33 degrees or less at the sketch scale. The epic look then comes from deep valleys and steep walls cut by rivers (R2) and ice (R2b), as on real mountains. Today's live terrain changes with the next TERRAIN_VERSION bump; it is not yet locked. R1 needs a fresh landform and geology export on Dave's PC.
  - *Checks.*
    - The R2 summary's `landform_steeper_than_its_rock_threshold.near_landform_peaks` near 0.
    - The first step's summit drop near 0.
    - At the chosen snapshot the island summit is within a few metres of its landform height and no peak on hard rock (granite, basalt) has lost more than 25 m (`landform_peaks.highest_ten`). Soft-rock secondary peaks may wear lower, as on real islands; that is variety, not a breach of the summit rule.
    - No round summits or ring cliffs: on the slope picture the steep ground round a tall summit follows its lobes and ridges, not a circle, and the granite outline in `inputs.png` (A3) is lobed.
- **A2 tectonic uplift field**: where and how fast the land rose. It comes from the macro landform (the existing "design uplift"), smoothed, so it varies only at the scale of the massifs. Nothing is added at hill scale: the first R1 run tried plane waves at 1 to 2 km and they showed as regular blobs, an arbitrary pattern (document section 11: "Variety should emerge from differing environmental conditions rather than arbitrary randomization"). The variety the rolling hills and detail noise gave today comes instead from the rock bands, the rain and the drainage itself. Fault blocks from `tile_geology` may later step the uplift, because that is a geological cause.
- **A3 rock**: `tile_geology` provinces, layers and features (`rock.py` already loads the layers). Per cell this gives erodibility (K) and the rock's water properties:
  - **aquifers and aquitards**: chalk and sandstone hold and pass water (porous aquifers); limestone and marble pass it through dissolved fractures (karst); basalt through joints and lava tubes; shale, slate, coal measures and most granite hardly at all. Springs and seeps form where a permeable bed lies on a tight one (document section 3), and the R1 map shows many such contacts in the folded bands, so spring lines along them are expected;
  - **soluble rock**: limestone, marble and chalk. Caves form mainly in limestone and marble; chalk is weak and porous, so it gives dry valleys, seasonal bournes and springs at its base, rarely caves;
  - **faults and joints** (`tile_geology` already places faults): weaker, more permeable zones, so valleys can follow them (rectangular drainage), springs can rise along them, and drainage can be redirected (document sections 3, 4 and 10).
  The first R1 map shows folded alternating hard and soft beds (sandstone, limestone, slate against shale, chalk, tuff). Where they strike across a massif, trellis drainage should emerge from the erosion by itself (document section 4). Hard beds make ledges, gorges and falls; soft beds make wide valleys (section 10). Numbers for permeability by rock type are researched before R4 (Gleeson et al. 2011 and the British Geological Survey aquifer classes are the starting points).
- **A4 climate**: rain from wind and relief by the linear orographic model (Smith and Barstad 2004). That model gives the rain rate while moist air is lifted, not a yearly total, so the yearly rain is the background plus the orographic rate times the share of the year with moist onshore flow (`storm_share`). The share is calibrated once against a real maritime peak (Ben Nevis, 1,345 m: 4,104 mm a year, observatory mean 1883 to 1904) and then fixed as a climate property. The first run, without it, peaked at 13,300 mm a year, which is beyond any temperate mountain of this height. Snow: the share of precipitation falling as snow comes from temperature, falling with height by the lapse rate; snowmelt makes the spring high flow (document section 8). Today `climate.py` reads moisture from a finished bake, which is circular. In the rebuild the climate is computed from A1 and the world air before any water.
- **A4 one climate per island, and the snow on it (8 Nov, the snow decision).** Kathleen's mountain art (#585) asks for summer snow patches on the starter summit, and Jamie's research (`docs/knowledge/snow-lines-mountain-climate.md`, #594) found two temperature models that disagree and neither gives any: world generation puts the starter at about 15 °C at sea level (the latitude curve in `world.json`), so its snowfield rule (`snow_max_c` -8 °C) starts near 3.5 km; the live weather (`src/shared/weather.gd`) uses 10 °C with a continental swing of ±14 °C. The coordinator chose a cooler, wetter starter island, which is real physics (Scotland, western Norway), not an art snow line. The design:
  - *One climate record per island*, set at the same island-type stage as `summit_m` (rule D-10): a sea-level annual mean, a seasonal swing and the orographic rain (this A4 model). World generation, this pipeline and the live weather all read it, so the snow the player sees, the temperature the warmth system reads and the biome under it agree. The starter island takes about 9 °C with a swing of about ±5 °C (Fort William at the foot of Ben Nevis; the Ben Nevis summit observatory read -0.3 °C over the year at 1,345 m, about 8.4 °C at sea level by the lapse rate). Other islands vary by type and latitude; small ocean islands keep small swings (±5 to 7 °C).
  - *Permanent snow and ice keep their rules* (`snow_max_c` -8, `glacier_max_c` -14): they are right for ice caps and the giant peaks. Lowering them to fake patches would put snow biomes on hills.
  - *A baked seasonal snow layer* (stage D record, R6; read at runtime in R7): for each cell the year phase when snow first lies and when it melts out. Snowfall comes from the precipitation and the daily temperature; drift moves it (lee cells within 45 degrees of downwind gain, crests lose: the rule-based model of Purves et al. 1998 on Aonach Mòr reproduced gully snow); faces steeper than about 45 degrees shed it to their foot; melt is by degree-days (about 5 mm of water per °C per day for snow, Hock 2003), less on shaded faces. Jamie's worked case gives, for 9 °C ±5.5, gully snow that lasts the year on a 1,450 m summit and open slopes bare by early summer: the patches sit at about 1,200 to 1,250 m, 80 to 85% of the summit, in the lee and the shade, as Britain's most lasting patches do (about 1,130 m on Ben Nevis, 1,140 m on Braeriach). The drift factors (about 2 to 3 in lee gullies, 0.3 to 0.5 on scoured crests) are assumptions until a run checks them.
  - *The cold stage against today.* B2's equilibrium line, 487 m, is the Loch Lomond Stadial's (Coire Breac). Weather sounds and the ground shader read the snow layer at the player's feet instead of one number for the whole view.
- **A5 runoff (new)**: one water budget, computed here and used by every later stage (principle 7). Evaporation by the Fu form of the Budyko curve (ET/P from the rain and the potential evaporation, 483 mm a year, the MORECS mean for Scotland; the Institute of Hydrology finds it changes little with height in such uplands), then the rest split by the rock into quick runoff and groundwater recharge by the rock's base flow index (UK Low Flow Studies, table 3.3: chalk about 0.94, old limestone about 0.5, mudstones and hard rocks about 0.4). Code: `tools/watershed/runoff.py`; values and sources in `params.json`. Stage B erodes with the quick runoff, so a valley is as large as the floods that really run in it; Stage C sends the recharge through the groundwater to springs, seeps and baseflow. Rocks the UK tables do not cover (marble, slate, granite, basalt, tuff) take the nearest class for now and are flagged for the R4 research pass.

### Stage B: landscape evolution (the ground)
Three parts, in the order the land would have lived through them.

**B1 the long river era.** Stream power with deposition (FastScape style: Braun and Willett 2013; deposition per Yuan et al. 2019), plus hillslope creep:
- erosion driven by the quick runoff of A5 accumulated downstream, not by the rain alone, so permeable and dry ground cuts less (principle 7);
- K from rock, and a channel threshold so weak flows do not incise ("no deep gullies under tiny flows"). The threshold is on the runoff-weighted area together with slope (the slope-area form of Montgomery and Dietrich 1988), not a fixed area, so wet hills carry more channels than dry or permeable ones (document sections 1 and 8);
- creep rate from the rock and its soil cover, and from the climate: valley spacing is set by the balance of creep and channel cutting (Perron et al. 2009), so one creep rate everywhere would space valleys evenly across unrelated catchments (a never-see item). R2 scales creep by the rock and cover (as `lem.py` did); a climate term waits for a source;
- sediment deposition where carrying capacity drops (inside bends, valley floors, lake entries, coast), so floodplains and deltas form;
- threshold hillslopes: past a threshold angle hillslopes fail by landslides, so their mean angle stays near it whatever the erosion rate (Burbank et al. 1996; Montgomery 2001). Linear creep has no such limit; run 624bd23c grew a ridge more than 1,000 m above its landform with 40 to 45 degree flanks. Each cell is therefore capped at the threshold slope (35 degrees over a 16 m cell) above its receiver inside the implicit solve, and what a slide removes joins the sediment carried downstream (talus and fans at the slope foot; the hybrid landslide model of Campforts et al. 2020 routes debris the same way). The threshold is set by rock, because hard rock holds steeper walls (Schmidt and Montgomery 1995): granite 57 degrees, basalt 48, slate and marble 44, limestone 42, sandstone 40, tuff 38, and 35 for shale, coal, chalk and under loose cover over 1 m. Granite's is measured: the steepest part of a cirque headwall has a median slope of 57 degrees over 1,593 cirques in nine regions, regional medians 48 to 68 (Evans and Cox 2015), and at 48 the ws580e glacial run lost 43 m off the summit where the cirque walls met. The others are the art goal's light fantasy exaggeration within real hard-rock walls (the Skye Cuillin, Glen Coe), so tall peaks stand as walls and are not cut back to soft-rock angles;
- run long enough to reach steady state, at 16 m over the nine tiles (8.3 M cells). The summary reports how fast the summit and the mean land height still change over the last tenth of the run; near zero means uplift and erosion balance. Steady-state relief scales with uplift over erodibility, so the uplift is calibrated once so that the final ground matches the landform's heights at the scale of the massifs (Sal's relief bands), rather than shaping the result afterwards. One scalar was not enough: run c3b1b846 (with the slope limit) had its summit at 835 m against 1,459 m but its mean land at 190 m against 155 m, because the flat-topped, broad uplift raised the flanks while the rivers cut the centres. So the uplift is scaled by the ratio of the landform to the previous result, both smoothed at the uplift's own scale (`evolve.py --calibrate`), which keeps hill-scale pattern out of the uplift (R1's rule).
- **Dissection, not steady state (8 Nov, after run 506dadff).** The massif-scale calibration matched (mean mismatch 9 m), yet the summit stood at 894 m against 1,459 m. A steady state keeps the landform only at the smoothing scale: with threshold slopes and the valley spacing set by D/K, no peak stands more than a couple of hundred metres above its valleys. Real islands of this kind (Arran, Skye, the Highlands) are not in steady state; they are uplifted surfaces that rivers and then ice are still cutting into. So B1 now dissects the landform as it stands (`uplift_share` 0: the landform is the land as uplifted) and the run length is chosen from snapshots by the checks: summits near the landform's, the main valleys reaching into the massif cores, and the drainage and slope figures. Island character (tall alpine, rolling, low) is set per island and seed at the landform stage (`summit_m` by island type in `worlds/base_world/plan/sketch_rules.json`, and the giant-peak roll); the erosion stage carves valleys into it and must not flatten it. The summary reports land height percentiles and the 2 km local relief on high ground against the landform's.
- **Measured creep (8 Nov, after run 11bbbcc9).** Dissection with D 0.15 m²/yr wore the island to a low plain (summit 680 m at 0.25 Myr, 167 m at 2 Myr) and took 200 m off the summit in the first 5 kyr step. That D was chosen only to keep D/K while K was raised for a quick steady state; it is 30 to 50 times measured soil creep (about 0.003 m²/yr, Roering et al. 1999). Here the valley spacing comes from the channel-head rule, not D/K, so D is a measured value again (0.005 m²/yr). The summary checks each landform peak above 600 m against the highest ground now within 200 m of it (the summit rule: valleys carved, peaks kept), and splits the height change into river and slides against creep, so the process that sets the peaks is known. If the peaks still fall, the candidates are an incision threshold (small steep headwater flows cannot cut bedrock, the document's "no deep gullies under tiny flows"; Snyder et al. 2003, DiBiase and Whipple 2011) and hard rock under the tall peaks at the geology stage.
- **Incision threshold and hollow fill (8 Nov, after run d9f89615).** With measured creep, creep moved only 1 to 6 m near the peaks; rivers and slides took 63 to 768 m, and every landform peak lost over 100 m by 0.15 Myr. Two changes follow:
  - a channel cuts bedrock only where its flow can move the boulders on its bed: erosion is K e (Q^m S − threshold), never below zero, on bedrock, with none on loose sediment over 1 m. Q^m S is the channel steepness index, and erosion rises with it far faster than linearly once a threshold is in (DiBiase et al. 2010; DiBiase and Whipple 2011). This is the document's "no deep gullies under tiny flows";
  - a hollow fills with sediment to its sill and no higher. The run built mounds hundreds of metres high, because a filled hollow rose above its sill, the cells behind it became a new hollow, and the mound climbed the valley.
  The main landform peak also lost 145 m in the first 2,500-year step. That is the slope limit cutting back a spire steeper than its slate can stand (44 degrees), so the summit rule needs the landform stage to build peaks within their rock's standing angle. The summary now reports the share of the landform that is steeper than its rock's threshold.
- **Cover over rock (8 Nov, after run 2bf3b20f).** With the landform built within its rock's standing angle (#580), the peaks held for 25 kyr (worst loss 16 m), but then the granite massif wore down faster than the soft rock around it: the main granite peak lost 907 m by 0.1 Myr. That is backwards, and the solve had a fault that explains it (the next run checks it with the cut by rock). Each cell eroded at the rate of its top layer for the whole 2,500-year step, so a few centimetres of loose cover (erodibility 5) let granite (0.08) erode 62 times too fast for the whole step, and every slide or creep deposit gave it a new few centimetres. Soft rock suffered far less (shale 1.5, about 3 times too fast). Now the river cuts through the cover at the cover's rate, with no threshold, and the rest of the step at the rock's rate, with the rock's threshold. The summary reports `cut_by_start_rock_m` (mean and 99th percentile cut by the rock at the start surface), where granite and basalt must show the least.
- **A rough start (8 Nov, after the 8a38328c zooms).** On a smooth landform every flow line runs straight down its face beside the next, and erosion deepens them side by side: the zooms showed flanks combed into straight parallel gullies, and granite, which wears least, kept the comb (drainage density 38). The ±0.5 m uncorrelated noise the run started with cannot turn a flow line on 16 m cells that drop 5 to 10 m each. The start is now the landform plus roughness with the spectrum of real ground (random phases, amplitude falling as wavelength^(H+1), H 0.8; measured topography is self-affine with H mostly between 0.5 and 0.9, Turcotte 1997), between 48 m and 1.5 km, scaled to a mean slope of 0.1 (a height spread of about 7 m, under 1% of the starter island's relief) and faded out below 20 m so the coast does not move (`evolve.start_roughness`). It is a starting condition, as in other landscape models: the valleys that branch from it are cut by the rivers, and the summary reports its size.
- **Walls fail along their whole height (8 Nov, after glacial run a3ffca14).** The threshold-slope cap lowered a cell only against the neighbour it drains to, chosen as the step starts. Beside a slot cut along one flow line of a smooth flank, the cells on either side drain in parallel, not into the slot, so they stood until a later step routed them there, one cell a step: run a3ffca14 left granite walls of 86 degrees and 270 m, and 1.3% of land steeper to a neighbour than any rock stands. Each step now first finds the highest each cell may stand (`_slope_ceiling`), and the solve holds the cell under it; what comes off is eroded volume and goes down the tree as slide debris. Under a glacier the limit is on the ground, not the ice surface (`lift`, the ice thickness). The limit is a true round cone within each rock: each cell carries the cell its limit hangs from and offers it to its neighbours (vector propagation, as for the landform's flank cones), and where the rock's threshold changes the limit is taken on from the neighbour, so a hard-rock wall stands on the soft slope below it. The first version carried the limit from neighbour to neighbour in straight and diagonal steps, an eight-sided distance, and glacial run bc02d1aa came out with every threshold face a flat facet edged along the compass and the diagonals and the summit massifs cut like crystals (its 6 km zooms).
- **Flow paths follow the slope, not the grid (8 Nov, after the 8a38328c, 4edbc722 and bc02d1aa zooms).** With steepest descent to one of eight neighbours, all the flow on a smooth face runs along one compass or diagonal direction, so faces were combed into straight parallel gullies set by the grid; the rough start bent them on gentle ground but not on faces near the threshold. Each cell now drains by D8-LTD (Orlandini et al. 2003): the true downhill direction is the steepest of the eight facets round the cell (D-infinity, Tarboton 1997), and of the two neighbours beside it the cell drains to the one that keeps the path's sideways drift, carried down the path, the smaller (`evolve.flow_paths`). There is still one receiver per cell, which the implicit solve needs, and the paths follow the real aspect, zig-zagging between neighbours. glacial.py, lakes.py and groundwater.py route the same way.
- **Channel heads from measured source areas (8 Nov, after run 6cb50039).** The drainage density was 17 km/km² overall and 47 on the wet granite faces, where nearly every cell was a channel and the lower flanks were combed with parallel gullies. Measured channel heads need more water: Tennessee Valley (760 mm of rain) has A = 1,978 tan θ^−1.65 m², source areas of 1,200 to 40,100 m² and a drainage density of 7.3 km/km² (Montgomery and Dietrich 1989); Rio Cordon has A S² = 511 + 16.6 h m² for an h m DEM, 776 m² at our 16 m (Tarolli and Dalla Fontana 2009). Scaled to our reference runoff, both give A S² of about 800 to 1,000 m², against our 450. So the rule is now 3.6 ha at a slope of 0.15 (A S² = 810 m², still weighted by the quick runoff, so wet ground has more channels), and a channel also needs 1,000 m² of plain drainage area (4 cells): below that, a "channel" is one cell's own runoff, which a 16 m grid cannot tell from a hillslope. Valleys stay where the water gathers; the faces between them are hillslopes, lowered by slides and creep as their channels deepen.
- *Breaking change:* `uplift_share` 0 means B1 no longer reaches a steady state, so `last_tenth_*` now measure how fast the ground is still being worn down, not a balance. The calibration (`--calibrate`) stays for runs with uplift.

A long river era fills closed hollows with sediment. That is right in nature too, and it means any hollow left at its end is not, by itself, a lake.

**B2 glacial history (new).** The island's climate has snowy winters, a treeline at 620 m and summits near 1,450 m. A landscape like that (western Scotland, Norway, coastal British Columbia) carries a recent glacial history on its high ground, and that history is where most of its lakes, many of its falls and much of its wetland come from. The document names it twice (sections 6, 10 and 11: "glacial depressions", "glacial history"). So after B1:
- an ice stage with a lowered snowline (the equilibrium-line altitude, a parameter): glaciers grow where snow outlasts melt and flow down the B1 valleys;
- glacial erosion rises with ice flux and sliding, so it is strongest where ice converges and is confined: bowl-shaped cirques at valley heads (tarns after the ice), U-shaped troughs, and overdeepened rock basins behind bedrock sills at confluences and narrows (Kessler, Anderson and Briner 2008; Alley et al. 2003);
- tributary valleys with less ice are left hanging above the main trough: natural falls with a cause;
- moraines where the ice ended dam valleys (moraine lakes); till and hummocky ground in the lowlands it reached (kettle ponds, poorly drained flats for fens and bogs); outwash below the ice margins (braided channels have a reason there);
- then a short post-glacial era (about 11,700 years) of river and creep work, too short to fill these basins, as in nature.
Where the chosen snowline puts no ice, there is no glacial imprint: glaciated and unglaciated catchments then differ by cause (document section 11). The model is chosen by research before any code (step R2b).

*Research so far (7 Nov):*
- **Model.** A glacial stream-power law (Hergarten 2021, Earth Surface Dynamics 9:937; benchmarked against the process-based iSOSIA ice model by Liebl et al. 2023, Geoscientific Model Development 16:1315, both in OpenLEM). Erosion is E = K_g A_i^m S^n, where A_i is the ice flux expressed as an equivalent catchment area and S is the slope of the ice surface. Ice flux comes from a mass balance that is linear in height above the snowline: below the snowline the ice melts, and ice production comes from the same precipitation field (A4). Fluvial and glacial erosion mix as K_eff = γ^m K_g + (1 − γ)^m K_f with γ = A_i / A. Glacier width scales as A_i^0.3. The finite-thickness version, with a constant thickness-to-width ratio, lets the ice surface stand above the bed. It has the same implicit, outlet-up form as our B1 solver, so it extends `evolve.py` rather than adding a second model, and it runs on millions of cells on a PC.
- **What it gives, per the benchmark:** hanging valleys and steps at confluences, in roughly the same places as iSOSIA; overdeepenings (the rock basins that become lakes); and flat-floored valleys from V-shaped ones, with some V left. Its stated limit is that it "cannot replace process-based models such as iSOSIA but is complementary to them". Cirques are not shown in the parts read so far, so their shape is checked on our maps before we rely on them.
- **Snowline.** For a stadial like Scotland's last one, a reconstructed equilibrium line at Coire Breac (eastern Grampians) is 487 m (Loch Lomond Stadial). Ours comes from the mass balance with the A4 precipitation, so the wet west builds more ice than the dry lee, which adds regional variety by cause.
- **Moraines.** Hergarten 2021 uses a shared stream-power law, E/K_d + Q_s/(K_t A) = A^m S^n (his eq. 3). It is the same erosion-deposition balance as our B1 solver, with K_d/K_t taking the place of G. The glacier carries sediment while its transport capacity lasts and drops it where the ice flux falls near the terminus, where meltwater may cut it again (eqs. 30 to 36: one sediment flux, carried by whichever of ice or meltwater spends less power). The paper does not build moraine ridges as such, so moraine dams are a result to check on our maps, not a promise.

*R2b method:*
1. Mass balance on the ice surface: the share of precipitation that becomes ice rises linearly from 0 at the snowline H_e to 1 at H_f, and is negative below H_e (melt). The precipitation is A4's field. Ice flux A_i accumulates down the routing like discharge until it reaches zero (the terminus).
2. Width w = a A_i^0.3 and thickness h = δ w. The ice flux is spread over a swath (his section 3). Every cell with ice is a start point c0 on its cardinal path, where each upstream point is the largest donor. Donors of c0 that are off the path join c0's swath if they lie within w_i/2 of an upstream path point c_i, and the search repeats outward through their donors. A swath cell takes the ice flux of its path point, and where swaths overlap the largest flux wins. Erosion then uses A_i,eff = A^0.25 A_i^0.75 (his eq. 22, ε = 0.25). The weighting by the unextended total flux A keeps a slightly parabolic valley floor and stops flow running in parallel lines down the flat floor.
3. Each step: add h to the ground, route on the ice surface (depressions in it filled), solve the same implicit erosion-deposition with K_eff = γ^m K_g + (1 − γ)^m K_f and γ = A_i / A, then subtract h.
4. Climate history: B1's long river era, then one cold stage with the snowline lowered (the parameter, starting near the stadial value), then the post-glacial era (about 11,700 years) of river and creep work with today's climate.
5. Outputs: each lake basin's cause (cirque, rock basin behind a sill, moraine or till dam), hanging-valley lips (fall candidates), till and outwash cover, and the ice extent, for R3 and R5.

*R2b as coded (`tools/watershed/glacial.py`, 7 Nov):* the method above, starting from B1's saved ground so the ice settings can be rerun alone. Choices the paper leaves open, each written in the code:
- the swath half-width is measured from each ice cell, walking up its donor tree, rather than from the nearest upstream path point; cells further up the valley get the swaths of the path cells there, which carry almost the same flux;
- the ice thickness comes from the effective flux A^0.25 A_i^0.75, so it thins toward the swath margins;
- the slope limit of B1 does not apply to ice cells (a glacier front is steep ice, not a hillslope). Ice-free walls are measured against the ice surface beside them, because the ice holds them up. After it melts, the post-glacial era lets oversteepened walls fail, as paraglacial rock slope failures do (a cause for landslide dams);
- the ice-free walls and the post-glacial era use B1's slope limit by rock, so hard-rock trough sides and cirque headwalls stand steep (up to 48 degrees in granite and basalt) while soft rock and till relax to 35;
- the uplift is the one B1 used (`uplift` in its ground.npz), so with B1 dissecting the landform (no uplift) the cold stage adds none either;
- the cold-stage water is the rain that does not become ice, plus melt where ice arrives, less a cold-stage evaporation. All of it is quick, because frozen ground makes even chalk impervious (Goudie 1990);
- land never goes below sea level, so no fjords: the coastline is fixed in this model.
Each hollow left after the post-glacial era is labelled ice-scoured floor, glacial deposit sill, other sediment sill, or none, for R3.

**B3 other lake makers, by condition.** Each is a named cause the lake table records:
- karst sinkholes (dolines) on limestone and chalk, where water dissolves the rock and drains down (closed, and explainable);
- landslide dams in steep valleys of weak rock, rare, where slope, relief and rock make a failure likely;
- oxbows and backswamps on meandering lowland reaches (stage C5);
- coastal lagoons behind bars where sediment supply is high and the shore is low (stage C8);
- beaver ponds belong to the ecology later (small, low-gradient streams near woodland); they are not made here.

The output is the **final ground**, not a delta, together with the routing graph it was eroded with. The final 8 m ground is the 16 m result refined, plus small hillslope roughness that cannot make pits (section 4.6).

### Stage C: hydrology on the final ground (the water)
- **C1 routing**: priority flood (Barnes et al. 2014) and steepest descent on the final ground. That gives basins, divides, receivers and rain-weighted discharge. This is the only routing.
- **C2 lakes**: every depression of the final ground is first given its cause from Stage B (cirque, glacial rock basin, moraine dam, kettle, doline, landslide dam, oxbow, lagoon). A hollow with no cause is a numerical leftover, a few decimetres of resampling, and is filled; it is not a lake. Each caused depression is then tested with a water balance (fill, spill and merge over the depression hierarchy: Barnes et al. 2020 and 2021): inflow (its catchment's runoff plus groundwater) against evaporation and seepage over its area. If inflow wins, it fills to its lowest saddle (the controlling spill point) and a river leaves there; full neighbours merge into one lake. If it never fills, it is a pond at its balance level, or a seasonal wetland. A closed basin needs that explanation, which makes them rare (document section 6, and "closed basins are exceptional").
  *R3 as coded (`tools/watershed/lakes.py`, 7 Nov):* the cause comes from R2b's hollow labels, and hollows with none are filled. The depression tree is built by visiting cells from the lowest up with a union-find (local minima start depressions, saddles join them, contact with the sea ends them). Fill-spill-merge settles it from the highest spill down, so a tarn's overflow reaches the lake below it. The inflow is the catchment's runoff from the one water budget, without the recharge of cave-forming rock (that comes back at a cave spring in R4 and R5). The loss is open-water evaporation (PET / 0.7, after Penman 1948) plus floor seepage. Seepage is a stand-in by rock until R4's groundwater solve gives each lake its exchange from the head. Oxbows (C5), lagoons (C8) and dolines (B3, which needs a dissolution step) are not yet in.
- **C3 groundwater**: a steady water-table solve on a coarse grid (64 m), with recharge from A5 and transmissivity from rock. The water table follows hydraulic head, not the surface routing, so a groundwater divide can sit off the surface divide, and karst conduits can carry water under a ridge to a spring in the next basin (document section 3). Where the water table meets the ground there is seepage: springs at slope breaks and where a permeable bed meets a tight one, broad seeps, and wetlands on flats (USGS; document sections 3 and 7). Each stream gets a baseflow share, and that sets perennial versus intermittent.
- **C4 channel heads**: a channel starts where drainage area times slope passes a threshold (Montgomery and Dietrich 1988). The threshold rises in dry or permeable ground, so heads sit higher in wet catchments and lower in dry ones. A head is then labelled by what feeds it: a seep or damp hollow (the common case), a spring, a wetland outlet, a lake outlet, or a seasonal rill. Ponds and caves are never added to make a head.
- **C5 network**: reaches between confluences. Discharge is the quick runoff from upstream plus groundwater gained, minus water lost where the bed lies above the water table (losing reaches on permeable rock, karst and alluvium), so a river can shrink as well as grow (document section 4). Each reach has discharge (mean, wet season, dry season), slope, flow regime (perennial, intermittent or ephemeral), width and depth from hydraulic geometry (width about Q^0.5, depth about Q^0.4: Leopold and Maddock 1953), and reach type from slope (cascade above about 0.065, step-pool 0.03 to 0.065, plane-bed 0.015 to 0.03, pool-riffle below that: Montgomery and Buffington 1997). Meanders only on pool-riffle reaches with room in the valley floor, their wavelength scaled to the channel width, so meanders differ between rivers. Falls only where the bed drops sharply over hard rock or from a hanging valley. Natural levees only on lowland alluvial reaches with fine sediment, low and broad (not the retired bank walls). More than one channel only in wetlands, deltas and braided outwash, where the conditions for it exist (a never-see item).
- **C6 wetlands**: cells that stay saturated (water table at or near the ground, low slope, convergent flow). They are typed by the document's section 7 and EPA definitions: marsh (surface water, soft plants), swamp (woody), fen (groundwater fed, peat), bog (rain fed, peat, acidic).

  *R4 method (C3, C4 and C6; drafted 7 Nov, before code):*
  1. **Grid and equation.** The water table is solved on a 64 m grid (4 by 4 model cells averaged) as steady unconfined flow under the Dupuit assumption, div(T grad H) + R = 0, with T = K b and one transmissive thickness b, so the system stays linear and is solved directly (scipy sparse). This is the approach of global water-table maps (Fan, Li and Miguez-Macho 2013, Science 339:940): recharge against lateral flow, with the land surface as a ceiling.
  2. **Boundaries.** The sea holds H = 0. R3's lakes hold their levels. Everywhere else the ground is a ceiling: a seepage-face iteration marks the cells where the solved table would rise above the ground, fixes H to the ground there, and solves again. A marked cell that would take water in rather than give it out is released. When the set stops changing, every marked cell is groundwater discharge to the surface: springs, seeps and gaining reaches. Whether a table follows the topography or the recharge then comes out of the solve, as Haitjema and Mitchell-Bruker (2005) describe, not from a rule.
  3. **Conductivity by rock** from Gleeson et al. (2011) classes, using K = k × 7.5e6 m/s for water near 10 °C:
     - limestone, marble and chalk: carbonate;
     - sandstone: coarse siliciclastic;
     - shale and coal: fine siliciclastic;
     - granite and slate: crystalline;
     - basalt and tuff: volcanic;
     - loose cover from Stage B: outwash and alluvium coarse, till fine.
     Cave-forming rock gets a karst factor, because carbonate permeability grows with scale where conduits connect (Gleeson et al. 2011). That makes its table low and its water converge on a few low outlets, the cave springs of C7.
  4. **Recharge** from A5 (runoff times the base flow index), on the rock at the final surface. The run prints the balance: discharge to the land plus outflow to the sea must equal recharge.
  5. **Outputs at 16 m**, interpolated from the 64 m head:
     - depth to the water table;
     - groundwater discharge per cell;
     - baseflow, the discharge accumulated down the surface routing. This gives a first regime: perennial where baseflow passes a small threshold, intermittent where only quick flow runs from a catchment big enough to carry a channel, and ephemeral otherwise. R5 adds the losses where a bed lies above the table.
  6. **Heads (C4).** The top cell of every Stage B channel is labelled by what feeds it:
     - spring: groundwater discharge within two cells above a spring threshold;
     - seep or damp hollow: smaller discharge;
     - lake outlet: at an R3 spill point;
     - wetland outlet: inside a C6 wetland;
     - seasonal rill: quick runoff only.
     No pond or cave is added to make a head.
  7. **Wetlands (C6).** A wetland cell is not lake, sea or channel, has its water table within a set depth of the ground, and lies on a low slope with converging or flat flow. Its type comes from what wets it (document section 7, EPA):
     - salt marsh: in the tidal band, joined to the sea, on sediment;
     - fen: fed by groundwater discharge;
     - bog: fed by rain only (no groundwater discharge), where rain is high and slopes gentle. Blanket bog needs about 1,000 mm of rain and 160 rain days a year, a figure still to verify;
     - marsh: on alluvium beside a river or lake, flooded from it;
     - swamp: marsh conditions below the treeline (620 m), on the drier fringe where woody plants hold.
  All thresholds go in `params.json` with their sources or flagged. The checks are the never-see items on wetlands (no wetland on freely draining steep ground) and on heads (no head without a supply).
- **C7 cave springs**: only where the rock lets water travel underground in open passages: limestone and marble (karst caves), fractured basalt (lava tubes, which `tile_rules` already defines) and fault zones; chalk mainly gives springs without caves. The cave catchment drains underground (sinkholes, a losing reach) and the outlet sits lower down where the passage meets the surface, perhaps in another surface basin. The spring's flow equals what that underground catchment receives, so no large river comes from a small opening without supply. Rare by construction.
- **C8 coast outlets**: from slope, sediment load and coast shape: a rocky outlet, a tidal creek, an estuary or a delta (document section 9).

  *R5 method (C5, C7 and C8; drafted 8 Nov, before code; numbers from Jamie's `docs/knowledge/rivers-floodplains.md`, #599, unless marked):*
  1. **Which channels are streams.** B1's channel map is where water cuts (density 9.8 km/km² on ws580e, 27 on granite); most of those cells are hollows a walker would call a dry gully. A visible stream is a channel whose wet-season flow passes a threshold, set so the island's density of perennial streams is about 2 to 2.5 km/km², as mapped in a wet slate upland (Plynlimon, 2.0 to 2.4 km/km²; Kirchner 2009, via Jamie's `docs/knowledge/upland-streams-numbers.md`, #612). Field networks there are 2 to 5 times longer, mostly seasonal gullies, so the rest of B1's channels are intermittent or ephemeral gullies, not blue lines. No Highland or Norwegian figure was found, so the one site stands for now. Each reach then gets its regime from R4: perennial where baseflow keeps it running, intermittent where only quick flow does, ephemeral otherwise. Where the bed lies above the water table on permeable rock, alluvium or karst the reach loses water to the ground (document section 4), so a stream can shrink and sink.
  2. **Flows.** Mean flow from the A5 budget (quick runoff plus R4 baseflow, minus losses). Wet-season and dry-season flows from the A4 rain's seasons and the snowmelt of the A4 snow layer. Bankfull flow, which sizes the channel, is the flood that returns about every 1.5 years, about 0.9 of the median annual flood QMED (0.9 judged). QMED over the mean flow follows the catchment (Flood Estimation Handbook, 2008): about 50 at 1 km², 36 at 10 km² and 25 at 100 km² on peat over granite or slate (Plynlimon, 10 km² of slate, reads 25 to 27), and about 22, 16 and 11 on permeable rock, falling as area^-0.15; a lake on the way (FARL 0.9) cuts it by about 30%. So small tight catchments get channels sized for big floods and look over-wide in a dry spell, by cause.
  3. **Size.** Width = C Q_bf^0.5 and depth from hydraulic geometry. For gravel-bed rivers like ours the UK fit of Hey and Thorne (1986, SI units) ties C to the banks: 4.33 with grassy banks, 3.33 with 1 to 5% trees, 2.73 with 5 to 50%, 2.34 above 50% or where incised. So the same flow runs narrower through woodland, by cause. Depth does not depend on the banks; the often-quoted Hey and Thorne depth could not be confirmed, so depth is Parker's (2007) d ≈ 0.24 Q^0.4.
  4. **Reach type** from slope (Montgomery and Buffington 1997): cascade above 0.065, step-pool 0.03 to 0.065, plane-bed 0.015 to 0.03, pool-riffle below.
  5. **Pattern** from numbers, never chance:
     - *braided* only where S Q_bf^0.44 > 0.0125 (Leopold and Wolman 1957), the bed is loose coarse sediment (R2b outwash, fans, scree aprons) and the banks are bare (above the treeline, fresh outwash);
     - *meandering* on pool-riffle reaches whose valley floor is wider than the meander amplitude plus one width. The planform is the sine-generated curve (Langbein and Leopold 1966): wavelength about 11 W (7 to 15 W per river, tighter in cohesive banks), amplitude about 2.7 W^1.1, bend radius at least 2 W, sinuosity = valley slope over the channel's stable slope, riffles every 5 to 7 W;
     - otherwise *confined*, nearly straight but never straight past about 10 W.
  6. **Floodplains** where a meander belt has room: the plain about one bankfull depth above the bed, levees about 0.2 depth high and one width wide (Albernaz et al. 2020), the backswamp lowest, where R4's floodplain marsh and swamp then sit. Old wide plains carry oxbow scars or lakes from cut-offs, each listed as a feature with its cause (C2's oxbows).
  7. **Resolution.** On 8 m cells a 4 m brook's meander (wavelength about 45 m) is under six cells long, so brook meanders live on the R7 reach polylines, and only rivers wider than about 8 to 10 m meander in the ground raster. The raster carries the meander belt and floodplain; the polyline carries the line the water takes.
  8. **Falls.** Only at a step the river cannot cut quickly: a hard bed over soft rock (the fall retreats 1 mm to 10 cm a year and leaves a gorge below it), a hanging valley's lip from R2b (tens of metres up to about a kilometre above the trunk), or a fault. In the final ground that is a reach more than 10 times steeper than the reach above, pinned at a rock contact, a trough side or a fault. One plunge only where one hard bed makes up about half the step or more; otherwise tiers and cascades down a steep bed (judged: no count of falls against cascades was found). Never a staircase of equal steps ("A waterfall never steps"). Each fall records its rock, its height and its cause.
  9. **Fans** are already made by B1's deposition where channels leave their walls; R5 checks them (surface 1.5 to 25 degrees, convex across, steepest at the apex) and records the live channel's path across each. **Terraces** come only from the end of the R2b cold stage (outwash fill cut by the Holocene river), so they are found in the result, not stamped; with a fixed sea there are no base-level terraces.
  10. **Cave springs (C7)** at R4's low outlets of karst and fractured-basalt rock, each with its underground catchment and flow. Every passage a player can see from inside is baked at a size LOD 2 can hold: at least 3.6 m half-width and half-height (`CAVE_MIN_VOXELS` x 4 m), keeping Dave's small, medium and large difference above that floor; a passage meant to be narrower is a dead end LOD 2 closes in full (the decided B1 in `docs/design/cave_lod_carving_design.md`). The generator then carves the baked size at every level, and `test_cave_lods` check 4 (the LOD 2 lip count, 221 today) must read 0.
  11. **Coast outlets (C8)** from each mouth's sediment flux (B1 and B2 record it) against the waves. A delta forms where the river's sand supply beats what the waves carry off: R = sand supply / (90 Hs^2.4 Tp^0.2) in kg/s, a delta where R > 1 (Nienhuis et al. 2015); deltas are common under 1 m mean wave height and nearly absent above (Caldwell et al. 2019). A small river brings about 0.01 to 0.2 kg/s of sand, while waves move about 2 kg/s at 2 km of fetch and about 100 at 50 km, so on our islands no river under about 500 km² builds an open-coast delta (judged from those numbers): the mouth is a rocky outlet, a beach-blocked mouth or an estuary where the coast is a drowned valley. Deltas form only in lakes and bays with under about 2 to 5 km of fetch; there, distributaries split at mouth bars within the backwater length (bankfull depth over bed slope; Ganti et al. 2014). R3's lakes get Gilbert deltas at their inflows (flat top, steep front).
  12. **The follow-the-water report**, the success test: for several basins, a profile from each head to the sea listing every feature met (head kind, reach types, falls, lakes, wetlands, springs, the outlet) with its cause and its seven answers (principle 8), and the never-see checks over the whole island.

### Stage D: the island water record
One record, written once per bake and read by everything:
- per-cell rasters at 8 m: ground, water class (sea, lake, river, wetland by type, seasonal), water level, discharge, flow regime, depth to water table, basin id;
- vector network: reaches (polyline, Q per season, width, depth, reach type, regime), heads (kind), confluences, falls, lakes (cause, level, spill point, outlet reach), springs and seeps, cave springs, coast outlets;
- per feature, the answers to the seven plausibility questions (principle 8);
- per reach and lake, what the water should look like (document section 12): bed material (boulders, cobbles, gravel, sand, silt, peat), sediment load (clear, cloudy after rain, silty), peat colour where fed from bogs, and turbulence class. The water and ground shaders read these later, so the look agrees with the hydrology.

Tiles are cut from it by world position, so seams agree by construction.

*Record layout, agreed with Steve's Stage E design (#541), 7 Nov:*
- rasters are raw little-endian grids at 8 m, one set per tile, (T+1)² samples with shared edge samples (the `export_raw_heights` convention);
- vectors live in one island-wide `record/network.json`, so a reach that crosses a tile edge stays one object with one id and is never clipped at a seam;
- `record/manifest.json` holds the record version, params, input hashes, origin and cell size. Units are metres and m³/s, in world x, z and y up;
- water class codes: 0 none, 1 ocean, 2 lake, 3 river (kept from today), then 4 marsh, 5 swamp, 6 fen, 7 bog, 8 seasonal channel, 9 seasonal pond, 10 salt marsh or tidal flat. Perennial, intermittent or ephemeral is carried by the regime raster;
- extra rasters: `fresh_m` (distance to perennial fresh water) and `sediment_m` (loose cover), both computed island-wide so seams agree;
- each reach carries wet-season, mean and dry-season flow, bed material and its downstream reach id. Karst sinks are listed with the id of the cave spring they feed;
- climate (A4): `record/climate.json`, one object per island id: `sea_level_mean_c` (starter 9.0), `seasonal_swing_c` (half the summer-to-winter range, on a cosine; starter 5.0), `warmest_year_phase` (0.375, the weather's convention), `daily_range_c` (full range, coldest at 05:00 and warmest at 15:00, Jamie's live cycle), `lapse_c_per_km` (6.5), `rain_mm_yr` (the island's background rain; the per-cell rain is the A4 field), and the weather spells, multi-day warm and cold departures from the season that bring the coast its winter frosts: `spell_amplitude_c` (the typical departure, ±; starter 4.0) and `spell_days` (how long one lasts, [3, 6]), run live as a random walk that relaxes back to the season over that time (Jamie's #620). The air at a place is mean plus the seasonal, spell and daily terms minus the lapse times height: one formula for the live weather, warmth, ice on still water and the tile biomes. The baked snow window (and the biomes) use the climate without spells, the long-run average; live lying snow comes and goes with the spells on top of it;
- snow (A4): two byte rasters at the 16 m model grid, `snow_on` and `snow_off`, the year phase (0 to 255 over the year, the weather's own year phase) when snow first lies and when it melts out. The window runs forward from `snow_on`, through the year's end if it must, to `snow_off`; equal values mean no lying snow, and `snow_on` 0 with `snow_off` 255 means snow all year (a patch that outlasts the summer). The client shows snow on a cell when the clock is inside its window, and footsteps and the ground shader read the same cell;
- ids are deterministic from the seed and params: stable within one record version, never kept across rebakes.

*Cold-air pools for valley fog (Jamie's spec, `docs/design/fog_pools_and_sea_fog.md` part A, #608; folded in 8 Nov):* on clear calm nights cold air drains downhill and fills low ground to its spill point like water (Clements, Whiteman and Horel 2003, Peter Sinks), so fog belongs in enclosed low ground at any height. The record carries it from the same terrain the water uses:
- *Closed pools* from R3's depression tree, run on the ground with lake surfaces as floors (the air lies on the water, so a full lake pools no air above it; a pond below its spill, a seasonal pond or a dry closed basin does). Kept at 10 m deep and 0.25 km² or more; each has `pool_id`, `spill_m`, `floor_m`, `area_m2`, its outline at `spill_m` and its cause.
- *Valley pools* along the R5 reaches: behind each narrows (valley floor width 20 m above the bed under half the width upstream) the cold air backs up to a level surface, the narrows' bed plus `valley_pool_share` (0.3) of the valley depth. Here the valley depth is the lower rim above the bed at the narrows, so no surface stands above the lower side of its valley (the air would spill over it) or above the next pool upstream's spill. Each stretch from one narrows up to where the bed rises through its surface is one flat sheet, listed in `network.json` with its reach id.
- *`fog_pool` raster*: depth below the cold-air surface, clamped at 150 m and scaled to a byte, on the 64 m groundwater grid.
- *Checks*: every pool has a floor (its deepest 10 m lies on ground under 15 degrees: a pool does not hang on a slope, though its sheet may meet steep walls); no surface above its spill or its valley's lower rim; every pool has a named cause.
- The 0.3, the 10 m and 0.25 km² minimum, the 150 m cap and the client's 60 m full-night depth are judged, not sourced, and are checked on Dino's pictures. The client draws one sheet per pool at min(spill, floor + 60 m × mist).

### Stage E: readers
- **Tile bake** (`tile_baker.gd`): heights and water come from the record. The rule channels and all eight repair passes are switched off and archived (section 6).
- **Runtime ground**: brooks narrower than a cell are drawn from the reach polylines at voxel scale (their real width and depth), not as 8 m trenches.
- **Live water** (the 2 m and 8 m sims): seeded from the record's levels and discharges. Players' ditches still change it, because the sim stays the truth at runtime.
- **Falls, water sounds, caves, climate, biomes, sites**: read the record instead of working the water out again.

*R6 method (Stages D and E on Sonny's side; drafted 8 Nov, before code; the readers are Steve's `docs/water/stage_e_readers_design.md`, #541):*
1. **One writer.** `tools/watershed/record.py` reads the post-glacial ground (B2), the lakes (R3), the groundwater (R4), the network, caves and outlets (R5) and the climate and snow (A4), and writes `<world>/record/` once, on Dave's PC. The manifest holds the record version, the code sha of every stage and the hash of every input; the bake refuses a record whose hashes do not match its world, as the setup cache does. The record is generated, so it stays out of git like the tiles.
2. **The window.** The model window must end at sea on every side (no land on its frame), so no river or groundwater divide is cut by its edge; the writer refuses a window that fails this. Tiles outside it keep today's pipeline until the rebuild runs over every island.
3. **Ground at 8 m**, in this order (section 4.6):
   - the 16 m post-glacial ground refined by a separable monotone cubic (Fritsch and Carlson 1980) along x and then z. It passes through every 16 m height and does not overshoot along either axis, so summits, ridges and channel beds keep their heights (a smoothing spline would lower the summits, and Catmull-Rom overshoots into pits and bumps);
   - rivers wider than about 8 m carved along their R5 polylines at their width and depth, inside the meander belt and floodplain the 16 m ground already holds, with banks at the bed material's angle; narrower brooks are left to R7, which draws them at voxel scale in the valleys B1 cut;
   - lakes at their R3 levels on the refined floor, with Gilbert deltas at the inflows R5 lists;
   - fine roughness at 8 to 32 m (the B1 start spectrum carried down) on hillslopes only, faded out near channels, lakes and the coast;
   - then a priority flood: any closed cell not inside a caused lake is a fault. The roughness is removed there and the cell listed, never filled silently.
4. **Rasters** per tile at 8 m with shared edge samples: ground and water level as 32-bit floats (the window reaches 2,250 m down the sea floor and peaks elsewhere reach 3,400 m, more than 16 bits hold at 0.1 m), water class, discharge (16-bit, log-coded), regime, depth to the water table (0.25 m steps), basin id, `fresh_m` and `sediment_m`; at 16 m the snow window; at 64 m `fog_pool`. About 60 MB a tile, so about half a gigabyte for the nine starter tiles. Code: `tools/watershed/record.py`, which writes the record without rivers and marks it incomplete until R5 exists.
5. **One switch.** `tile_rules` gains `water_source` ("record" or "rules"). With "record" the bake takes heights and water from the record and skips the rule channels and the repair passes (section 6); with "rules" it bakes as today, so a bad record can be rolled back until R8 archives the old code. Steve's M2 is the reader switching its source; nothing else in the game changes at that step.
6. **TERRAIN_VERSION** rises once, and the nine starter tiles are rebaked on Dave's PC.
7. **Checks before success** (section 5): the follow-the-water report over several basins; every water cell with a cause; no closed cell outside a caused lake; the 8 m summit within a metre of the 16 m one; the spawn re-chosen by the spawn search on the new ground, near the resources (Dave, 24 Oct and 6 Nov: move the spawn, never adjust heights or place things for it). The sketch's `spawn_keep`, which keeps the old land round the spawn out of the coast profile and foothills, is then an area exception with no cause and is retired with the other passes; David4's headless gates on the rebaked tiles; Dino's pictures at Dave's standing points.

*R7 method (runtime; drafted 8 Nov, before code). The live water stays the truth at runtime (a dug ditch changes where water goes); the record is where it starts and what the drawing and sounds read where nobody has changed anything:*
1. **Seeding.** When the 8 m field (`src/water2`) and the 2 m regions (`src/sullivan_water/live/dig_windows.gd`) open a tile, they start from the record's water levels instead of spinning up from rain: lakes at their levels, rivers at the surface their reach depth gives, the sea at 0. Inflow at the window's edge is each crossing reach's discharge; springs and seeps put in their record flows; all sources take the season's share (wet, mean or dry) from the record, so a drought exposes intermittent beds while spring-fed streams run on (section 4.5).
2. **One friction.** Today the 8 m field uses one Manning roughness for the whole field. It takes each cell's from the record's bed material instead (boulders, cobbles, gravel, sand, silt, peat; values from the USGS guide of Arcement and Schneider 1989, to check against its table), the same bed the R5 depth assumes. The check: an untouched tile, after a spin-up, holds its rivers within about 10 cm of the record's levels. If it does not, the record's depth or the friction is wrong, and the report says which reaches; nothing is nudged to agree.
3. **Brooks under a cell wide** (most of the network): the voxel generator carves each from its R5 polyline at its width and depth, with its meander planform, as one shape at every collision level and never widened per level (the cave lesson, `docs/design/cave_lod_carving_design.md`); a seam test like `test_cave_lods` must count no lips along brook trenches. Its water is drawn as a ribbon along the polyline at its width and record surface (the way falls are drawn today), the 8 m sheet stepping aside under it with the same soft `fine_blend` edge; inside a 2 m region the 2 m water draws it instead.
4. **Falls** come from the record's list (lip, height, width from the flow, plunge or tiers or cascade, rock, cause), drawn from the lip down the face to the plunge pool. `water_falls.gd`'s search of steep drops in the 8 m levels stays only for falls players make (a ditch dug over a cliff), so a fall appears where the ground has one, not where an 8 m cell is steep.
5. **Lake surfaces** are flat sheets at the record level, outlined by the shore. The live field holds them through their outlet spill, so a lake drains only through its outlet (or a player's cut).
6. **Sounds** read the record: falls by height and flow, rapids by reach type (cascade and step-pool loud, pool-riffle quiet), a lake by its shore. That ends the open bug of a waterfall roar over a calm lake by cause: the roar needs a fall in the record within earshot.
7. **Snow and fog** are data for the weather owner: the snow window per cell at the player's feet and in view, and one fog sheet per cold-air pool (Jamie's part A). R7 gives them through `tile_terrain_data.gd`, as Steve's design does for `fresh_m`.
8. **Checks:** walking the island (Dino's standing points and the follow-the-water basins): every brook continuous from head to sea in the 2 m drawing, the 8 m drawing and at every terrain level, with no straight line where they meet (the BUG-006 rule); no fall or roar without a record fall; the levels check of item 2; and the cost inside the measured budget (1 tile 49 ms, 4 tiles 189 ms a simulated second, against 250).

## 4. Details that decide whether it looks right

### 4.1 Hills that drain
Hills come from uplift variation that is then eroded (A2 and B), so every hillside has its own drainage. No closed bump is ever added after the erosion. This replaces `tile_rolling.gd` and the hill passes in `tile_height.gd`.

### 4.2 Channels sized by supply
Bed width and depth follow discharge (C5). A seep rill is centimetres to a metre; a lowland river is many metres. Valley shape comes from Stage B: V-shaped where steep and hard, broad floodplains where gentle and depositional. Not every steep brook gets a floodplain.

### 4.3 Lakes that spill where they should
The outlet is always the lowest saddle (C2). The level comes from the balance, so a lake never overflows through an arbitrary higher shore.

### 4.4 Headwaters that are mostly subtle
Most heads are seeps, damp hollows, and rills that merge. Spring pools exist only where groundwater discharge is strong enough to keep one. Caves exist only per C7.

### 4.5 Seasons
The record keeps wet-season, mean and dry-season discharge, and the regime. Later the live sim can scale its sources by season. Then drought exposes intermittent beds and lowers shallow ponds, while groundwater-fed springs persist (document section 8).

### 4.6 Fine detail without pits
The 8 m ground is the 16 m result refined with a smooth interpolation. It adds roughness only on hillslopes away from channels, and that roughness is depression-checked: a priority flood after it must show no new closed cells, or the roughness is removed there. Channel beds come from the reach polylines, at the reach's width and depth.

### 4.7 The document's note on architecture
The document says it describes natural behaviour, not a replacement architecture, and asks Claude to preserve the architecture where it works. Dave's assignment then authorises redesigning the terrain generator and the water "wherever necessary", while reusing what genuinely supports the new approach. I read the two together: the generation order is rebuilt because it is the cause of the failures (section 1), and everything else in the game's architecture is kept: Voxel Tools terrain, the tile format and bake, the geology and climate rules, the 2 m and 8 m live water, and the readers, which switch to one record. Old tools that place features by chance are not reused: `lem.py`'s random "tarn" sink patches and `meander.py`'s random meanders.

## 5. How we will validate (no success by "it runs")

Every check runs on Dave's PC (Dino or David4), never in the cloud.

1. **Never-see checks, automated** (one test per bullet of the document's list), for example:
   - no reach whose discharge does not grow at a confluence;
   - no head without a supply in the record;
   - no water level above its own spill;
   - no pond on ground steeper than a threshold without a dam (barrier) cell;
   - no wetland on freely draining steep ground;
   - no flow uphill: every reach strictly descends, with falls flagged;
   - incision depth scaled to discharge (no deep cut where Q is tiny);
   - no single shape repeated across catchments (variation statistics for meander wavelength and fall spacing);
   - closed basins counted and each one explained;
   - every lake has a named cause from Stage B (no leftover pits as lakes);
   - every feature answers the seven questions (principle 8);
   - confluences point downstream (junction angles acute, not T-shaped);
   - valley spacing and channel density differ between catchments in step with their rock and rain, not evenly across the island;
   - rain stays within real bounds for the climate (the calibration peak in `params.json`).
2. **Follow-the-water report.** A tool that picks heads in several contrasting basins and walks each to its destination. It prints, at each step, supply, discharge, width, slope, reach type, tributaries joined and destination, and writes a profile picture. Dave reads these.
3. **Overview maps**: basins coloured, network by order, lakes and wetlands, heads by kind, groundwater depth.
4. **Ground pictures by Dino** at chosen points in contrasting basins, and in the old trouble spots from Dave's play test.
5. **Seams**: the existing edge_match check, 0 hard failures.
6. **Godot 4.7.2 and Voxel Tools**: the tile format and the runtime reader stay compatible; any change is versioned (TERRAIN_VERSION bump) and listed in section 7.
