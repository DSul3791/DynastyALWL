# Dynasty: A Life Well Lived — How Natural Watersheds Work

**Purpose:** A natural-history reference for Claude to apply to Dynasty's existing interconnected procedural world systems. This is **not** a request to build a new generator, change architecture, or implement isolated water features. Claude decides how these principles fit the existing systems.

## The central principle

Waterways are connected outcomes of climate, landform, rock, soil, groundwater and time. Rain and snow fall across a drainage basin; some water evaporates or is used by vegetation, some infiltrates and becomes groundwater, and some travels over or near the surface. Gravity moves water toward lower elevations. Small flow paths converge into larger ones, ultimately reaching lakes, wetlands, rivers, the sea, or internally drained basins. Rivers also reshape the terrain they cross. A believable island must reflect these relationships consistently.

## 1. Drainage basins and divides

A **watershed** (drainage basin) is the area that contributes water to a shared outlet. High ridges and saddles often form drainage divides. Nearby raindrops on opposite sides of a divide can end up in different rivers. Each tributary has a smaller sub-basin nested within a larger basin. Not every valley needs a permanent stream: drainage area, rainfall, infiltration, slope, and groundwater determine whether flow persists.

On an island, most surface drainage ultimately reaches the coast, but it may first pass through ponds, lakes, marshes, or underground conduits. Small closed depressions may lose water by evaporation or infiltration instead of having a surface outlet.

## 2. How headwaters actually begin

There is no universal single 'starting point' for a stream. Common origins include:

- **Diffuse seepage:** Moist soil on a slope gradually becomes saturated; tiny trickles merge into a channel.
- **Groundwater springs:** Water emerges where the water table intersects the land surface, or where permeable material meets a less permeable layer.
- **Wetland outlets:** Water drains from a saturated meadow, marsh, fen, or pond into a small channel.
- **Storm and snowmelt channels:** Shallow channels flow only during wet periods and may be dry most of the year.
- **Lake outlets:** A stream exits at the lowest effective spill point of a lake or pond.

A headwater is often a patch of damp ground, several small rills, or a barely visible trickle—not a dramatic cave or perfectly circular pool. A brook can begin without a pond, and a pond can exist without feeding a permanent brook.

## 3. Groundwater and springs

Groundwater is water occupying pores and fractures beneath the surface, not usually a giant underground lake. Rainfall infiltrates where soil and rock allow it; groundwater moves from higher hydraulic head to lower hydraulic head, often much more slowly than surface flow. The groundwater divide may not exactly match the surface divide.

Springs can appear on hillsides, at the base of slopes, along faults or fractures, or where permeable layers meet less permeable layers. A **seep** may emerge over a broad patch; a **spring** may have a recognizable outlet. Some springs run year-round because aquifers store water; others weaken or dry during drought.

**Cave springs** are especially associated with fractured or soluble rock, such as limestone karst. Water entering sinkholes, fractures, and underground passages can emerge at a cave mouth or resurgence lower down. A cave spring should be an occasional geological consequence, not the standard origin of every river. Underground flow paths may cross surface drainage boundaries.

## 4. Why tributaries join into rivers

Small channels generally converge downhill into larger channels. A river's discharge tends to grow downstream as tributaries and groundwater add water, though it can shrink where water infiltrates, evaporates, is diverted, or enters a losing reach. A stream's width, depth, and flow usually reflect its contributing basin and seasonal water supply—not merely its distance from a source.

Drainage patterns depend on geology and topography: branching **dendritic** networks are common in fairly uniform material; **trellis** networks form in alternating resistant and weak rock; **radial** drainage can descend from isolated highlands; **rectangular** patterns may follow fractures. Confluences tend to be downhill and are usually angled in the direction of flow rather than perfect T-junctions.

## 5. How channels shape the land

Steep headwaters often cut narrow valleys and move coarse stones. On gentler slopes, channels may meander across wider floodplains, eroding outer banks and depositing sediment on inner banks. Floods can overtop banks, deposit silt, create side channels, and leave oxbow lakes. Waterfalls and rapids tend to occur where gradient, rock resistance, or abrupt changes in bed elevation justify them.

A meander is not random decoration: it develops where channel slope, sediment, bank resistance, and valley width permit lateral movement. Bedrock-confined mountain streams may be relatively straight or step-pool shaped; low-gradient alluvial rivers may wind substantially. Not all rivers meander and not all valleys have broad floodplains.

## 6. Why ponds and lakes exist

Water accumulates where a basin retains it. Examples include glacial depressions, blocked valleys, landslide dams, beaver dams, abandoned river bends, bedrock hollows, and groundwater-fed basins. Their levels reflect inflow, rainfall, groundwater exchange, evaporation, infiltration, and outflow.

A lake with a surface outlet typically spills at a controlling low point; it does not drain over arbitrary sections of its shore. A lake without an outlet may persist where inflow balances evaporation and seepage. Some ponds are seasonal. Headwater ponds are possible but should not be mandatory at every stream source.

## 7. Why wetlands form

Wetlands form where soil remains saturated or flooded for long enough to influence vegetation and soil conditions. Typical settings include floodplains, valley bottoms, shallow lake margins, groundwater discharge zones, coastal flats, and poorly drained depressions. Wetlands are not simply shallow lakes with reeds.

- **Marsh:** Usually herbaceous vegetation in shallow water or saturated ground.
- **Swamp:** Woody vegetation (trees or shrubs) in wet ground.
- **Fen:** Groundwater-fed peatland, often receiving mineral-rich water.
- **Bog:** Rain-fed peatland, commonly acidic and nutrient-poor.

A wetland may filter and slow flow, spread it across multiple channels, store floodwater, and release water gradually. Some wetlands have no obvious open-water channel.

## 8. Weather, seasons and flow persistence

Water budgets vary with precipitation, snowmelt, temperature, vegetation, soil moisture, and aquifer storage. Heavy rain raises streams, sometimes after a delay; floodplains and wetlands temporarily store water. In drought, shallow ponds and intermittent streams may dry while aquifer-fed springs and larger rivers continue. Snowmelt can create a seasonal high-flow period. Local climate matters: a wet coastal slope may sustain streams that a dry sheltered slope cannot.

Streams can be **perennial** (normally year-round), **intermittent** (flow during wet seasons), or **ephemeral** (flow chiefly in direct response to rain/snowmelt). These are natural differences, not failures of a water system.

## 9. Coastal outlets and unusual cases

Most island rivers enter the sea at an estuary, tidal creek, delta, or rocky outlet depending on slope, sediment and tidal conditions. Near the coast, tides may influence water levels and reverse local currents. Some streams disappear into porous ground or karst before re-emerging; others terminate in internally drained wetlands or ponds. These should be explained by the setting rather than scattered randomly.

## 10. How water and terrain shape each other over time

A mature watershed is not a collection of channels laid over unchanged ground. Long-term runoff concentrates in low areas, incises channels, transports sediment, and gradually organizes the landscape into ridges, hollows, tributary valleys, and main valleys. The visible terrain should therefore support the drainage network and also show evidence of having been shaped by it.

- **Upper slopes:** Small rills, shallow hollows, exposed bedrock, narrow gullies, and steep channels appear where concentrated runoff can erode soil and rock. Not every hollow carries water permanently.
- **Mountain valleys:** High-energy streams can form confined V-shaped valleys, cascades, plunge pools, and step-pool channels. Their course is constrained by rock, slope, and existing valley structure.
- **Middle valleys:** As slope decreases and tributaries add water, valleys often broaden; channels transport gravel and sand, form bars, and erode banks. The channel does not have to sit exactly at the geometric center of the valley.
- **Lowlands:** Broad floodplains, natural levees, abandoned channels, backwaters, and wetlands can develop where water and fine sediment spread during floods.
- **Deposition:** Where flow loses carrying capacity—inside bends, at tributary mouths, entering lakes, or approaching some coasts—sediment accumulates rather than being eroded away.
- **Geological controls:** Resistant rock may create ledges, gorges, and waterfalls; softer layers may produce wider valleys. Faults, joints, glacial history, and landslides can redirect drainage.

The resulting terrain should look as though the drainage system has a history. Avoid carving uniformly deep trenches under every stream, making all banks identical, or applying dramatic erosion where flow is too weak to justify it.

## 11. Why neighboring watersheds look different

Adjacent drainage basins can develop distinct characters even on the same island. Differences arise from catchment area and shape, rainfall exposure, elevation, bedrock and fractures, soil permeability, vegetation, glacial or landslide history, and local slope.

- A steep, thin-soiled catchment may produce flashy rocky torrents with little wetland storage.
- A forested catchment with permeable soil and a productive aquifer may sustain gentle, clear, year-round brooks.
- A broad, poorly drained basin may have marshy headwaters, ponds, slow channels, and extensive floodplain wetlands.
- A fractured or karst basin may lose surface water underground and discharge it at one or more springs.
- A coastal basin with a short steep descent may reach the sea through a narrow rocky mouth; a low-gradient basin may form tidal wetlands or an estuary.

These are *possible outcomes*, not fixed biome templates. Variety should emerge from differing environmental conditions rather than arbitrary randomization. Within a basin, tributaries should still share a coherent regional geology and climate.

## 12. Visible evidence that water belongs in the landscape

A believable waterway changes the appearance of the ground and vegetation around it. These clues should agree with water energy, duration, sediment, and season:

- **Small seep or spring:** Damp soil, darkened rock, moss, moisture-loving plants, and subtle trickles rather than an oversized water jet.
- **Rocky headwater:** Exposed stones, small plunge pools, scour around obstacles, foam or turbulence at real drops, and coarse sediment.
- **Forest brook:** Root-exposed banks in eroding bends, leaf litter and woody debris caught at obstacles, gravel or sand patches, and riparian vegetation.
- **Meandering river:** Eroded outer bends, point bars on inner bends, occasional cutoffs and backwaters, and floodplain surfaces at believable heights.
- **Lake or pond:** Irregular shores shaped by basin geology, gradual shallow margins where appropriate, sediment or organic accumulation in quiet coves, and a physically plausible spill point if it overflows.
- **Wetland:** Saturated soils, hummocks and shallow pools, vegetation suited to long-lasting wetness, and diffuse or sluggish flow where appropriate.
- **Seasonal channel:** A recognizable bed or drainage hollow that may be dry, with deposited debris or scour showing where stormwater travels.
- **Coast:** A transition suited to local slope and sediment supply—rocky outlet, tidal channel, estuary, or delta rather than one universal river-mouth shape.

Water should not always be crystal clear, uniformly blue, or equally turbulent. Depth, sediment, bed material, organic matter, current, and lighting can affect appearance. Visual cues must support the underlying hydrology rather than mask contradictions.

## 13. Natural plausibility questions for every connected water feature

1. What area supplies its water, and by what pathways?
2. Why does water collect or emerge here instead of somewhere nearby?
3. What controls the water level and the downstream outlet, if any?
4. What kind of terrain, rock, and soil would produce this channel or basin?
5. How would it behave in a storm, normal weather, and drought?
6. What signs of long-term erosion, sediment deposition, or saturation should be visible?
7. How does it connect to the larger watershed and ultimately the coast or an internal sink?

**Guidance to Claude:** Treat these as globally applicable natural rules for the existing procedural systems, not a checklist of individually placed features. Preserve the current architecture and choose any implementation changes only after reviewing the code and official design. The aim is hydrological coherence across the entire island.

## 14. What We Expect to Have

This section defines the expected *world outcome*, not a request for a new architecture. The same natural principles should operate throughout Dynasty's existing interconnected procedural island systems. The result should be recognizable at ground level and from an overhead view.

### What we expect to encounter

1. **Credible headwaters:** Damp hollows, scattered seeps, small springs, rain-fed rills, wetland drainage, and occasional pond or cave outlets. Some headwaters should be seasonal; many should be subtle rather than spectacular.
2. **A connected hierarchy:** Tiny trickles merge into brooks; brooks feed streams; streams feed larger rivers where drainage area and water supply justify the growth. Tributaries join in geographically sensible directions.
3. **Distinct mountain and lowland water:** Steep channels with stones, rapids, and occasional waterfalls transition toward wider, slower, sometimes winding valley rivers with appropriate sediment and floodplains.
4. **Natural ponds and lakes:** Water occupies defensible depressions with plausible inflow, seepage, storage, and—where present—an outlet at the controlling spill point. Not every headwater requires a pond.
5. **Wetlands in the right places:** Marshes, swamps, fens, and bogs occur where saturation and water chemistry make sense, including valley bottoms, floodplains, groundwater discharge zones, and poorly drained depressions.
6. **Rare but convincing cave springs:** Underground-fed outlets appear where rock structure and catchment support them, not as a routine explanation for major rivers.
7. **Land shaped by water:** Channels occupy believable valleys; erosion, gravel bars, floodplains, damp soils, and vegetation transitions provide visible evidence of long-term water action.
8. **Meaningful regional variety:** Different catchments produce different combinations of rocky torrents, gentle forest brooks, lakes, marshes, and coastal outlets, while remaining consistent with their local conditions.
9. **Believable changing conditions:** Rainfall and snowmelt raise flows; drought exposes some streambeds or lowers shallow ponds; groundwater-fed features persist longer where aquifer storage permits.
10. **Sensible destinations:** Most island drainage ultimately reaches the coast through a plausible outlet, sometimes passing through lakes, wetlands, or underground routes. Closed basins are exceptional and physically explainable.

### What we should never see

- A large river suddenly appearing from a tiny hillside opening without an adequate upstream or underground supply.
- A stream running uphill or crossing a drainage divide without a plausible geological explanation.
- A river of constant width and depth despite major tributaries, changes in slope, or changing water supply.
- Every stream beginning at a circular head pond or cave mouth.
- Identical meanders, identical waterfalls, or equally spaced tributaries repeated across unrelated catchments.
- A pond perched on a steep slope without a natural barrier, or a lake overflowing through an arbitrary higher shoreline.
- Wetlands placed on freely draining steep ground without a reason for persistent saturation.
- Deep erosion gullies under tiny flows, or wide flat floodplains along every steep mountain brook.
- Rivers branching repeatedly across ordinary uplands without the specific conditions that support distributaries or braided channels.
- Water and surrounding ground that visually disagree—for example, a permanent marsh with dry upland vegetation everywhere, or a fast torrent with no channel scour or coarse bed material.

### How we will judge success

**The follow-the-water test:** Pick a small spring, seep, or wet hollow and follow its drainage downhill. At each point, its water supply, channel size, terrain, tributaries, sediment, and eventual destination should make sense. Repeat this in several contrasting watersheds. From above, the full island should show coherent drainage basins; on foot, individual features should feel naturally formed rather than manually placed.

**Expectation for Claude:** Evaluate these outcomes against the existing game systems and official design. Decide how best to apply the hydrological principles globally. This document specifies the natural-world behavior and observable results; it does not authorize a replacement architecture or prescribe implementation.
